# Episode 2 · Why Do Firms Exist?

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome back! Episode two. Today's question sounds almost silly: why do firms exist at all?

HOST B: [playfully] Because people need jobs? Because someone wanted to get rich?

HOST A: [laughs] Good guesses! But the lecture gives a more precise answer. Let's start with the basic economic problem. Our needs and desires regularly exceed the resources available.

HOST B: There's never enough of everything.

HOST A: Exactly. And firms are there to coordinate value creation, to meet that demand with limited resources.

HOST B: Okay, but why in firms? Why not everyone just working alone?

HOST A: Because of the division of labour and specialisation. This goes back to Adam Smith, in "The Wealth of Nations", in 1776. When people split up the work and each person specialises, productivity shoots up.

HOST B: Why exactly?

HOST A: Two reasons the lecture names. Economies of scale: the advantages of size. And economies of learning: the advantages of practice. If you do the same task a thousand times, you get really good and fast at it.

HOST B: And the examples were fun.

HOST A: Yes! Henry Ford introduced the assembly line in 1913. And a Formula One pit stop is the perfect modern example. Every person has exactly one job, and together they change four tyres in about two seconds.

HOST B: [impressed] Two seconds. Okay, specialisation works.

HOST A: And in the spirit of Adam Smith, the first business studies were all about productivity. Frederick Taylor wrote "The Principles of Scientific Management" in 1911. The idea: business success is driven by patterns in human behaviour that you can identify.

HOST B: So business administration is a science?

HOST A: An empirical science, yes. It looks for patterns, and tries to find insights that apply generally. Typical questions: why are some companies more profitable than others? How do employees work together? How do managers decide? How do customers react to offers? And how do competitors act?

HOST B: Okay. But here's the puzzle from the slides. If specialisation is so great, why isn't there just one giant company that makes everything?

HOST A: [excited] That's the big question! The slide showed the largest Swiss companies: Roche, Novartis, Nestlé, ABB, UBS. Huge, but none of them makes everything.

HOST B: So what stops them?

HOST A: The answer is transaction costs. And it starts with Ronald Coase, who won the Nobel Prize in 1991.

HOST B: What's a transaction cost?

HOST A: Every transaction costs something beyond the price. The lecture lists five costs: initiation, negotiation, execution, monitoring, and adjustment.

HOST B: Can you make that human?

HOST A: Sure. Say you need a website. Initiation: you search for a designer. Negotiation: you agree on price and details. Execution: they build it. Monitoring: you check it's right. Adjustment: you fix things when they're not.

HOST B: [laughs] And every step costs time and money.

HOST A: Exactly. And here's the trick. In neoclassical economics, you assume there are no transaction costs at all. Perfect markets: identical goods, lots of buyers and sellers, nobody has market power, and everyone has complete information.

HOST B: Which never happens.

HOST A: Right. So Coase says: whether something happens through the market, buying from outside, or inside the firm, making it yourself, depends on where the transaction costs are lower.

HOST B: That's the make or buy decision.

HOST A: Exactly. Market means buy. Hierarchy means make. If you buy, you pay the transaction costs: searching, negotiating, monitoring. If you make it yourself, you pay other costs: internal coordination, inefficiency because there's no competitive pressure, and risk taking.

HOST B: So it's always a trade-off.

HOST A: Always. And the more complex the transaction, the more attractive making it yourself becomes.

HOST B: Quick check for the listener. According to Coase, when does a firm make something itself instead of buying it?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: When doing it inside the firm is cheaper than the transaction costs of buying it on the market.

HOST B: Now Tesla!

HOST A: Yes. Tesla builds its own chips and huge battery factories, called Gigafactories, even though specialised suppliers could do it cheaper. Why?

HOST B: Hmm. Control?

HOST A: Partly! Tesla is vertically integrating along the value chain. Elon Musk said in 2020 that Tesla designs and builds much more of the car than other carmakers, who mostly buy from the traditional supplier base. He called that "catalog engineering."

HOST B: And there are types of integration.

HOST A: Three. No integration: you only do your own step. Forward integration: you also take over the next step, downstream, closer to the customer. Backward integration: you take over the step before you, upstream, closer to raw materials.

HOST B: So Tesla making its own batteries is...

HOST A: Backward integration. And integration brings economies of scope, producing related things together more cheaply, and economies of learning.

HOST B: Pause and answer. A coffee roaster starts opening its own cafés. Forward or backward integration?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Forward integration. It moves closer to the end customer.

HOST B: Okay, so what decides how high transaction costs are?

HOST A: Three things. Asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency.

HOST B: One by one, slowly.

HOST A: Asset specificity: how much an investment can only be used for this one deal. Like a machine that only makes parts for one specific customer.

HOST B: Uncertainty?

HOST A: How hard it is to predict future conditions, actions or outcomes of the deal.

HOST B: And frequency?

HOST A: How often the transaction is repeated.

HOST B: And the scary one: the hold-up problem.

HOST A: [seriously] Yes. When asset specificity is high, one side makes a special investment. After that, the other side can behave opportunistically. It can demand to renegotiate, because it knows switching would be too costly for you.

HOST B: So you've built the special machine, and now your one customer says "lower your price, or we walk".

HOST A: Exactly. You're stuck. That's hold-up.

HOST B: And the lecture used game theory for this.

HOST A: Yes, a little game. Player A is a supplier deciding whether to invest in a specific machine. Player B is a carmaker deciding later whether to exploit the supplier.

HOST B: What are the payoffs?

HOST A: If A doesn't invest, A gets five, from its second-best option, and B gets five from its alternative. If A invests and B plays fair, both get ten. But if A invests and B exploits, B gets twenty and A gets zero, the scrap value.

HOST B: [thinking] So what happens?

HOST A: You solve it backwards. That's called backward induction. Start at the end. If A has invested, B can choose ten by being fair, or twenty by exploiting. B exploits.

HOST B: And A knows that.

HOST A: Right. So A compares: invest and get zero, or don't invest and get five. A doesn't invest.

HOST B: [surprised] But if both cooperated, they'd each get ten!

HOST A: That's the tragedy. The result, called the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium, is: B would exploit, so A doesn't invest. And that's the underinvestment problem. Good investments don't happen, because the supplier fears being exploited.

HOST B: Quick quiz. In the hold-up game, what's the equilibrium?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: B exploits, so A doesn't invest. That's the underinvestment problem.

HOST B: And back to Tesla. How does this explain the Gigafactories?

HOST A: All three factors point to "make". Asset specificity: the batteries are tailored to Tesla's own cars and energy products. Uncertainty: the technology and demand change fast, so it's hard to write complete contracts. Frequency: Tesla needs a huge number of battery cells, all the time. And hold-up risk: there are only a few specialised battery makers, so Tesla would depend heavily on them.

HOST B: So making them itself saves more transaction costs than a supplier could save on price.

HOST A: Exactly. And other carmakers are now copying this, planning to make more key parts themselves.

HOST B: There was a teaser for the next lecture too.

HOST A: Yes, Oliver Williamson, Nobel Prize in 2009. He showed that between market and hierarchy, there's a middle: hybrid forms. As asset specificity rises, you move from market, to hybrid, to hierarchy. Next episode is all about that.

HOST B: And the lecture's punchline?

HOST A: Three ideas. Firms are value creation engines. The division of labour drives productivity. And firms constantly decide make or buy, weighing efficiency gains against transaction costs.

HOST B: The textbook has something extra here, right? Theories of the firm.

HOST A: Yes. It lists five "theories of the firm", each with a different survival question. Neoclassical theory: does the firm have the right size to produce efficiently? Transaction cost theory: are the firm's boundaries in the right place? Resource-based theory: does it have resources that others can't copy? Behavioural theory: can it keep its stakeholders together? And customer value-based theory: does it create enough value for customers?

HOST B: And real firms have to answer all five at once.

HOST A: Exactly. The textbook also has a nice transaction cost example: hospital cleaning. Writing a contract for it would be really complex, with surgery schedules, special quality standards and liability. So doing it in-house can be cheaper.

HOST B: Final quiz! Name the five types of transaction costs.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Initiation, negotiation, execution, monitoring, and adjustment.

HOST B: Name the three factors that drive transaction costs.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency.

HOST B: What's the difference between economies of scale and economies of learning?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Scale is the advantage of size: average costs fall as you produce more. Learning is the advantage of practice: costs fall as experience grows.

HOST B: [warmly] Great. Next time: hybrid forms, alliances, joint ventures, and how platforms like Airbnb create value.

HOST A: See you there!
