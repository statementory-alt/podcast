# Episode 2 · Why Do Firms Exist?

HOST A: [curious] Here's a strange question. Why doesn't one giant company just make everything?

HOST B: Like... one company for cars, food, banking, phones, all of it?

HOST A: Exactly. If being big is so efficient, why don't we have one super-company?

HOST B: [laughs] Honestly, I've never thought about that.

HOST A: And that's the question behind today's episode. Welcome back to the Business Administration study podcast. This is episode two: why do firms exist?

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Here's the plan. First, the basic economic problem that firms solve. Then, why splitting up work makes us so productive. Then the key idea of the whole lecture: transaction costs, and the "make or buy" decision. Then Tesla, and a little game that shows a big danger called hold-up. And at the end, a quiz.

HOST A: And as always, one idea at a time.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Let's start with the basic economic problem.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: The lecture puts it in one sentence. Our needs and desires regularly exceed the resources available.

HOST A: In plain words: we always want more than there is.

HOST B: Exactly. More food, more housing, more phones, more holidays. But there's only so much labour, land, money and materials.

HOST A: So somebody has to organise how we use those limited resources.

HOST B: And that's what firms do. Firms coordinate value creation, to meet demand with limited resources.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay, but why do we need firms for that? Why not everyone working on their own?

HOST B: Because of one of the oldest ideas in economics: the division of labour.

HOST A: Adam Smith.

HOST B: Adam Smith, in "The Wealth of Nations", in 1776. His point: if you split a job into small steps, and each person specialises in one step, the group produces far more than if everyone did everything alone.

HOST A: Why exactly?

HOST B: The lecture gives two reasons. The first is economies of scale. That's the advantage of size.

HOST A: Meaning?

HOST B: When you produce a lot, your fixed costs, like a factory or a machine, are spread over many units. So each unit gets cheaper.

HOST A: Okay. And the second?

HOST B: Economies of learning. The advantage of practice. If you do the same task a thousand times, you get faster and better at it.

HOST A: So, size and practice.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And the examples were fun.

HOST B: Yes. Henry Ford introduced the moving assembly line in 1913. Each worker did one small step, and cars suddenly became cheap enough for normal families.

HOST A: And the modern example?

HOST B: A Formula One pit stop. Around twenty people, each with exactly one job. One takes off a wheel, one puts a new one on, one tightens it. And together, they change all four tyres in a couple of seconds.

HOST A: [impressed] Which no single person could ever do.

HOST B: That's the division of labour in one picture.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: The lecture also says this is where business studies started.

HOST B: Right. Frederick Taylor wrote "The Principles of Scientific Management" in 1911. He studied workers and tasks very precisely, to find the most productive way to do each job.

HOST A: And his big idea?

HOST B: That business success comes from patterns in human behaviour, and that we can find those patterns scientifically.

HOST A: So business administration is a science.

HOST B: An empirical science. It looks at real data, finds patterns, and tries to draw general lessons. Typical questions: why are some companies more profitable than others? How do employees work together? How do managers decide? How do customers react? How do competitors act?

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. So specialisation is great. Back to our opening question. Why isn't there one giant company that makes everything?

HOST B: The lecture shows the biggest Swiss companies. Roche, Novartis, Nestlé, ABB, UBS.

HOST A: Huge companies.

HOST B: Huge. But none of them makes everything. Nestlé doesn't build its own trucks. Roche doesn't run its own power plants.

HOST A: So something stops companies from growing forever.

HOST B: And that something is called transaction costs.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay, this is the key idea, so let's go slowly. What's a transaction cost?

HOST B: Every time you buy something from someone else, you pay the price. But you also pay extra costs, just for making the deal happen.

HOST A: Like what?

HOST B: The lecture lists five. Let's use an example. Imagine you need a new website for your shop.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: First, initiation. You have to search for a web designer and compare offers.

HOST A: That takes time.

HOST B: Second, negotiation. You agree on the price, the deadline, the details.

HOST A: More time.

HOST B: Third, execution. The designer actually builds it, and you exchange emails and files.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: Fourth, monitoring. You check if it's done right.

HOST A: And fifth?

HOST B: Adjustment. When something isn't right, you fix it, change the contract, or argue about it.

HOST A: [laughs] I've lived through all five of those.

HOST B: [laughs] We all have. Initiation, negotiation, execution, monitoring, adjustment. Those are transaction costs.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And classic economics ignores these, right?

HOST B: Yes. Neoclassical economics imagines perfect markets. Identical goods. Lots of buyers and sellers, so nobody has power over prices. Everyone has complete information. And no transaction costs at all.

HOST A: Which is not the real world.

HOST B: Not at all. And that's where Ronald Coase comes in. He won the Nobel Prize in 1991.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: What did Coase say?

HOST B: He asked: if buying things on the market is so efficient, why do firms exist at all? And his answer was: because using the market has costs. Transaction costs.

HOST A: So sometimes it's cheaper to just do things yourself.

HOST B: Exactly. Coase's rule is simple. A firm does something inside, or buys it outside, depending on where the costs are lower.

HOST A: That's the "make or buy" decision.

HOST B: Yes. Buying from outside is called "market". Doing it yourself is called "hierarchy", because inside a firm, a boss can just tell people what to do.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So "make" has no costs?

HOST B: No, it has different costs. If you make it yourself, you pay for internal coordination, like managers and meetings. You also lose the pressure of competition, so your own department might get lazy and inefficient. And you take on the risk yourself.

HOST A: And "buy" means paying the five transaction costs.

HOST B: Exactly. So it's always a trade-off. And the lecture says: the bigger, broader and more complex the transaction, the more attractive it becomes to make it yourself.

HOST A: Because complex deals are expensive to organise through contracts.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. According to Coase, when does a company make something itself instead of buying it?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: When doing it inside the company is cheaper than the transaction costs of buying it on the market.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Now Tesla.

HOST B: Tesla is a great example. It builds its own chips, and huge battery factories called Gigafactories, even though there are specialised suppliers who could probably make them cheaper.

HOST A: So why bother?

HOST B: That's the lecture's question. And the first part of the answer: Tesla is vertically integrating.

HOST A: Let's explain that slowly.

HOST B: Think of the value chain as a line. Raw materials on the left, the final customer on the right.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: No integration means you only do your own step in that line. Backward integration means you also take over the step before you. Upstream, towards the raw materials.

HOST A: And forward integration?

HOST B: You take over the step after you. Downstream, towards the customer.

HOST A: So Tesla making its own batteries is...

HOST B: Backward integration. Batteries come before the car.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Your turn, listener. A coffee roaster opens its own cafés. Forward or backward integration?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Forward integration. It's moving closer to the customer.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And Elon Musk said something about this.

HOST B: Yes, in 2020. He said Tesla designs and builds much more of the car than other carmakers, who mostly buy parts from the traditional suppliers. He called that "catalog engineering".

HOST A: [laughs] Like ordering a car from a catalogue.

HOST B: Exactly. And other carmakers are now following Tesla, and plan to make more key parts themselves.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay. So what decides how big the transaction costs are? When is buying risky?

HOST B: The lecture names three factors. Asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency.

HOST A: One at a time.

HOST B: Asset specificity. That's how much an investment can only be used for one specific deal.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: Imagine a supplier builds a special machine that only makes door handles for one specific car model. If that customer leaves, the machine is almost worthless.

HOST A: So high specificity means you're stuck with that partner.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Next: uncertainty.

HOST B: How hard it is to predict the future of the deal. If technology and demand change fast, you can't write a contract that covers everything.

HOST A: And frequency?

HOST B: How often the transaction happens. If you buy something once, a contract is fine. If you buy it every day, those negotiation costs add up.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now the scary part. The hold-up problem.

HOST B: Yes. Let's go back to our supplier with the special machine.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: Before the machine is built, the supplier and the carmaker agree on a fair price. Then the supplier invests, and builds the machine.

HOST A: And now?

HOST B: Now the carmaker knows the supplier is stuck. The machine can't be used for anyone else. So the carmaker says: "Lower your price, or we'll leave."

HOST A: [surprised] That's not fair!

HOST B: It's not fair, but it's possible. That's the hold-up problem. When asset specificity is high, after one side has invested, the other side can behave opportunistically and demand to renegotiate, because switching would be too costly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And the lecture turns this into a game.

HOST B: Yes, a little bit of game theory. Don't worry, it's simple.

HOST A: [laughs] Okay.

HOST B: There are two players. Player A is the supplier. Player B is the carmaker. First, A decides: invest in the special machine, or not. Then, if A invested, B decides: play fair, or exploit A.

HOST A: And what do they get?

HOST B: If A doesn't invest, both use their second-best options, and each gets five.

HOST A: Five and five.

HOST B: If A invests and B plays fair, both get ten.

HOST A: Ten and ten. That's the best for everyone.

HOST B: But if A invests and B exploits, B gets twenty, and A gets zero.

HOST A: Ouch.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Now, how do we solve this? We use backward induction. That means we start at the end, and work backwards.

HOST A: Okay. The last decision is B's.

HOST B: Right. A has already invested. B can play fair and get ten, or exploit and get twenty. What does B do?

HOST A: B exploits. Twenty is more than ten.

HOST B: Now go one step back to A. A knows that if it invests, B will exploit, and A will get zero. If A doesn't invest, A gets five.

HOST A: So A doesn't invest.

HOST B: Exactly. The solution, which is called the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium, is: B would exploit, so A doesn't invest.

HOST A: [thoughtfully] But that's sad. If they trusted each other, they'd both get ten.

HOST B: That's exactly the lesson. It's called the underinvestment problem. Good investments don't happen, because the supplier is afraid of being exploited later.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. In the hold-up game, what's the equilibrium?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: The carmaker would exploit, so the supplier doesn't invest. That's the underinvestment problem.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Now let's go back to Tesla, and put everything together.

HOST B: Okay. Asset specificity: Tesla's batteries are tailored to its own cars and energy products. So they're highly specific.

HOST A: Uncertainty?

HOST B: Very high. Battery technology and demand change fast, so it's hard to write a complete contract.

HOST A: Frequency?

HOST B: Huge. Tesla needs an enormous number of battery cells, all the time. Negotiating that again and again would be expensive.

HOST A: And hold-up?

HOST B: There are only a few specialised battery makers. So Tesla would depend on them, and they could demand to renegotiate.

HOST A: So all four point the same way.

HOST B: Exactly. Making batteries itself saves Tesla more in transaction costs than a supplier could save on price.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And there's a teaser for the next lecture.

HOST B: Yes. Oliver Williamson, who won the Nobel Prize in 2009, said there's something in between market and hierarchy. Hybrid forms, like alliances. We'll cover that next episode.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: The textbook adds one more useful idea here. Theories of the firm.

HOST B: Yes. The course book lists five theories, and each one asks a different question about whether a company can survive.

HOST A: Go slowly.

HOST B: Neoclassical theory: is the company the right size to produce efficiently? Transaction cost theory: are its boundaries in the right place, what to make and what to buy? Resource-based theory: does it have resources others can't easily copy?

HOST A: And the other two?

HOST B: Behavioural theory: can it keep all its stakeholders together? And customer value-based theory: does it create enough value for its customers?

HOST A: And real companies have to answer all five at once.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: The book also has a nice example of transaction costs.

HOST B: Yes: hospital cleaning. It sounds simple to outsource. But cleaning has to fit around surgery schedules, quality needs differ between areas, staff need special training to deal with patients, and liability must be clear.

HOST A: So the contract would be really complicated.

HOST B: Exactly. And that's why many hospitals clean in-house.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up the key ideas.

HOST B: One: firms coordinate value creation, because our needs are bigger than our resources. Two: the division of labour makes us productive, through economies of scale and economies of learning.

HOST A: Three: every market transaction has costs. Initiation, negotiation, execution, monitoring, adjustment. Four: firms make or buy, depending on where the costs are lower. And five: high asset specificity creates the hold-up problem, which can lead to underinvestment.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Final quiz. Name the five types of transaction costs.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Initiation, negotiation, execution, monitoring, and adjustment.

HOST B: What are the three factors that drive transaction costs?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency.

HOST B: What's the difference between economies of scale and economies of learning?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Scale is the advantage of size: costs per unit fall as you produce more. Learning is the advantage of practice: costs fall as experience grows.

HOST B: And Tesla building battery factories. Forward or backward integration?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Backward integration.

HOST B: [warmly] Great job. Next time: alliances, joint ventures, and how platforms like Airbnb and WhatsApp create value.

HOST A: See you there!
