# Episode 3 · Alliances, Networks and Platforms

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome to episode three! Today: lecture two, "New forms of organizing".

HOST B: [cheerfully] Last time we learned firms choose between making and buying. Market or hierarchy.

HOST A: Right. And today's point is: more and more, value is created by companies working together. That's neither pure market nor pure hierarchy.

HOST B: The hybrid!

HOST A: Exactly. Oliver Williamson's picture, from 1985. As asset specificity increases, the best form changes. With low specificity, the market is cheapest, and you mainly pay the cost of contracting. In the middle, the hybrid, you pay the cost of cooperation. And with high specificity, the hierarchy wins, and you pay the cost of organizing.

HOST B: Market, hybrid, hierarchy. Contracting, cooperation, organizing.

HOST A: Perfect. Now, hybrids come in different strengths. The lecture lines them up from closest to the market to closest to hierarchy.

HOST B: Go.

HOST A: First, the consortium. Several partners pool their resources for a joint project, often a really expensive one. The example is the Airbus consortium.

HOST B: Next?

HOST A: The non-equity alliance. A cooperation based only on contracts, with no ownership involved. Things like licences, supply agreements, or research partnerships. The example is Siemens and NVIDIA.

HOST B: And the equity alliance?

HOST A: Also a contract, but now one or both partners buy shares in the other. The example is Renault, Nissan and Mitsubishi.

HOST B: Then joint venture.

HOST A: A new company that the partners set up together, with shared ownership and shared risk. The example is Stellantis and CATL, building batteries together.

HOST B: And at the hierarchy end?

HOST A: Merger and acquisition. Two firms become one, or one is absorbed into the other. The example was Commerzbank and UniCredit.

HOST B: And there's a definition of strategic alliance to learn.

HOST A: Yes, from Gulati, 1995. [slowly] A strategic alliance is a purposive relationship between two or more legally independent firms, that involves exchanging, sharing or co-developing resources or capabilities, to jointly fulfil tasks and achieve mutually relevant benefits.

HOST B: The key part being "legally independent".

HOST A: Exactly. They stay separate companies.

HOST B: Quick check for the listener. Two companies create a brand new company together and share ownership. What's that called?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: A joint venture.

HOST B: Okay. But if partners don't own each other, what holds an alliance together?

HOST A: Three mechanisms. First, contracts: explicit rules about who contributes what, who gets what, how to exit, and how to settle disputes.

HOST B: Second?

HOST A: Trust and reputation. Repeated interactions build trust and reliability over time.

HOST B: And third?

HOST A: Relational norms. Shared values and norms that make it clear what fair behaviour is, even in situations nobody planned for.

HOST B: There was a great quote about this.

HOST A: Yes. Dr. Sahin, co-founder of BioNTech, about working with Pfizer on the Covid vaccine. He said trust and personal relationships are so important because everything was going so fast. They still only had a term sheet, and not a final contract, on many things.

HOST B: [surprised] They built a vaccine without a final contract?

HOST A: Pretty much. That's the power of trust.

HOST B: Then there was a part on termination rights. This comes from the professor's own research, right?

HOST A: Yes, Hanisch, 2025. Termination rights decide who can end an alliance, and when. There are three levels. No termination provisions: you can only terminate if the other side seriously breaches the contract. Otherwise, you can only claim damages.

HOST B: Second level?

HOST A: Conditional termination rights. You can end it for specific reasons both sides agreed on in advance.

HOST B: And third?

HOST A: Unconditional termination rights. You can end it for any reason, or no reason at all, maybe with a notice period. The slide had a real clause: Grünenthal could terminate the agreement for any or no reason with a hundred and eighty days' written notice.

HOST B: That sounds great for whoever has it.

HOST A: It's a trade-off. On one side, safeguarding and flexibility. On the other side, contracting costs and instability.

HOST B: And the research found something about money.

HOST A: Yes. In one thousand five hundred and seventy-six alliance contracts, higher upfront payments came with a higher chance that the paying client firm had an unconditional termination right. If you pay a lot upfront, you want the right to walk away.

HOST B: Pause time. Name the three mechanisms that govern alliances.

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Contracts, trust and reputation, and relational norms.

HOST B: Now networks. Companies don't just work in pairs.

HOST A: Right. Ritter and Gemünden, 2003, give five levels, from small to big. An interaction: a single exchange. A dyad: all the interactions between two actors. A portfolio: similar relationships a firm has with other organisations. A net: all the relationships of one actor. And a network: the whole structure of an industry or market.

HOST B: Interaction, dyad, portfolio, net, network.

HOST A: Exactly. And digital technology is a big reason networks have grown. The lecture quoted Ocasio and colleagues: the old world was Chandler's big business, diversified, vertically integrated, with slow markets. Today's world is fast-moving, entrepreneurial, high-tech firms with innovative business models.

HOST B: Which brings us to platforms!

HOST A: Yes. Digital platforms reduce transaction costs in three ways. First, match making: connecting buyers and sellers who'd struggle to find each other, with algorithms, filters and recommendations. That cuts search costs.

HOST B: Second?

HOST A: Market making: setting the rules, prices and standards so deals happen smoothly, and balancing supply and demand with pricing and access rules. This avoids adverse selection.

HOST B: And third?

HOST A: Trust building: ratings, reviews, verification, guarantees, dispute resolution. This minimises moral hazard.

HOST B: Can you explain adverse selection and moral hazard simply?

HOST A: Sure. Adverse selection is a problem before the deal: you can't tell good offers from bad ones. Moral hazard is a problem after the deal: the other side might behave badly once you've committed.

HOST B: Got it. And one-sided versus two-sided platforms?

HOST A: A one-sided platform mostly sells its own content to users, like Disney Plus. A two-sided platform connects independent suppliers, called complementors, with customers. Like booking dot com connecting hotels and travellers.

HOST B: Quick quiz. Ratings and reviews on a platform. Which function is that, and which problem does it reduce?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Trust building. It reduces moral hazard.

HOST B: Now network effects.

HOST A: Network effects are demand-side economies of scale. A product becomes more valuable the more people use it. Like WhatsApp: more useful the more of your friends are on it.

HOST B: And the other "economies" for comparison?

HOST A: Economies of scale: average cost falls as you produce more, like Amazon's warehouses. Economies of scope: making related products together is cheaper, like Unilever sharing distribution and research across brands. Economies of learning: costs fall as experience grows, like Tesla's battery costs.

HOST B: And there are two types of network effects.

HOST A: Direct network effects: value grows with more users on the same side, like a messenger app. Indirect network effects: value grows with more users on the other side. More hosts on Airbnb make it better for guests, and more guests make it better for hosts.

HOST B: And there's maths! [laughs] Don't panic, listeners.

HOST A: [laughs] It's easy. Direct network effects: with n users, the number of possible connections is n times n minus one, divided by two. With eight users, that's eight times seven, divided by two: twenty-eight.

HOST B: And indirect?

HOST A: m times n. With four suppliers and four customers, that's sixteen.

HOST B: And intermediation?

HOST A: From Baligh and Richartz, 1964. Without a middleman, every supplier deals with every customer: m times n transactions. With a middleman, everyone only deals with the middleman: m plus n. So the saving is m times n, minus m plus n.

HOST B: Let me try that. Ten suppliers, ten customers. Without a platform?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Ten times ten, a hundred transactions. With a platform, ten plus ten, so twenty. That saves eighty.

HOST B: [excited] Nice. And then winner takes all.

HOST A: Yes. Network effects create a positive feedback loop. More users attract more providers, and more providers make it better for users, so the platform grows fast. The strategic problem: it's hard for newcomers to enter, and markets can concentrate, with lock-in and winner-takes-all dynamics.

HOST B: Can anything stop that?

HOST A: Four counterforces. Multi-homing, meaning using several platforms at once, like drivers on both Uber and Bolt. Incompatibility or compatibility between systems. Data portability, taking your data with you. And regulation.

HOST B: And the punchline?

HOST A: Companies collaborate to create value. Cooperation needs governance mechanisms. And platforms help govern collaboration at a huge scale.

HOST B: Final quiz! In Williamson's picture, which form has the cost of cooperation?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: The hybrid.

HOST B: Direct versus indirect network effects: what's the difference?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Direct: value grows with more users on the same side. Indirect: value grows with more users on the other side.

HOST B: What are the counterforces to winner-takes-all?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Multi-homing, compatibility, data portability, and regulation.

HOST B: [warmly] Great. Next time: strategy! Porter's five forces, and why some industries make so much more money than others.

HOST A: See you there!
