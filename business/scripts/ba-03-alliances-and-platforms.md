# Episode 3 · Alliances, Networks and Platforms

HOST B: [curious] Quick question. How did BioNTech and Pfizer develop a Covid vaccine in less than a year?

HOST A: Lots of scientists working very hard?

HOST B: [laughs] Yes. But also: two separate companies working together, at incredible speed. And the co-founder of BioNTech later said they still didn't have a final contract on many things.

HOST A: [surprised] Wait, really? They did it without a contract?

HOST B: Pretty much. And that's where today's episode starts.

HOST A: Welcome back to the Business Administration study podcast. Episode three: alliances, networks and platforms.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Here's the plan. First, a quick recap of make or buy, and the "third way" in between. Then the five types of partnerships between companies. Then, what holds a partnership together. Then networks. And finally platforms, like Airbnb and WhatsApp, with some easy maths.

HOST A: One step at a time.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Quick recap from last time. Firms decide: make or buy.

HOST A: Make means doing it yourself, inside the firm. That's called hierarchy. Buy means getting it from outside. That's called the market.

HOST B: Exactly. And today's big idea is: more and more, companies create value by working together. Which is neither pure market, nor pure hierarchy.

HOST A: Something in between.

HOST B: A hybrid.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: This comes from Oliver Williamson, right?

HOST B: Yes, from 1985. He drew a simple picture. On one axis, asset specificity. Remember: how much an investment only works for one specific partner.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: When asset specificity is low, the market is cheapest. You just buy. The main cost is writing contracts. Williamson calls it the cost of contracting.

HOST A: And when it's very high?

HOST B: Then hierarchy is cheapest. You do it yourself. The main cost is running the organisation. The cost of organizing.

HOST A: And in the middle?

HOST B: The hybrid. Companies cooperate. And the main cost is the cost of cooperation.

HOST A: So: market, hybrid, hierarchy. Contracting, cooperation, organizing.

HOST B: Perfect.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay. Now the types of partnerships. The lecture lines them up like a scale, from closest to the market, to closest to hierarchy.

HOST B: Five steps. Let's take them one by one.

HOST A: Step one.

HOST B: The consortium. Several partners pool their resources for one joint project, usually a really expensive one.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: Airbus started as a consortium of European aircraft makers. Building a plane is too expensive and risky for one company alone.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Step two.

HOST B: The non-equity alliance. The partners cooperate only through contracts. Nobody buys shares in the other.

HOST A: What kinds of contracts?

HOST B: Licences, supply agreements, research partnerships. The example is Siemens and NVIDIA working together on technology.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Step three.

HOST B: The equity alliance. Still a contract, but now one or both partners also buy shares in the other.

HOST A: So they own a piece of each other.

HOST B: Exactly. That ties them closer. The example is Renault, Nissan and Mitsubishi.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Step four.

HOST B: The joint venture. The partners create a brand new company together. They share the ownership, and they share the risk.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: Stellantis and CATL, building a battery factory together.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And step five, the closest to hierarchy.

HOST B: Merger and acquisition. Two companies become one, or one buys the other and absorbs it. The example was Commerzbank and UniCredit.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let me repeat the scale. Consortium, non-equity alliance, equity alliance, joint venture, and M and A.

HOST B: From loosest to tightest.

HOST A: Your turn, listener. Two companies set up a brand new company together and share the ownership. What's that called?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: A joint venture.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a definition of a strategic alliance to learn.

HOST B: Yes, from Gulati, 1995. [slowly] A strategic alliance is a purposive relationship between two or more legally independent firms, that involves exchanging, sharing or co-developing resources or capabilities, to jointly fulfil tasks and achieve mutually relevant benefits.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: The key words being "legally independent".

HOST B: Exactly. In an alliance, the companies stay separate. That's what makes it different from a merger.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. But if the partners don't own each other, what holds the alliance together?

HOST B: The lecture names three mechanisms.

HOST A: One.

HOST B: Contracts. Written rules about who contributes what, who gets what, how to leave, and how to settle disputes.

HOST A: Two.

HOST B: Trust and reputation. If you work together again and again, you learn to rely on each other.

HOST A: And three.

HOST B: Relational norms. Shared values about what's fair. They help most in situations nobody planned for, where the contract says nothing.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And that's our BioNTech story.

HOST B: Exactly. Dr. Sahin said trust and personal relationships were so important, because everything was going so fast. They had a term sheet, a short summary of key points, but not a final contract on many things.

HOST A: So trust filled the gap that the contract couldn't.

HOST B: That's it.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. What are the three mechanisms that hold alliances together?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Contracts, trust and reputation, and relational norms.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Next, termination rights. This is from the professor's own research, right?

HOST B: Yes, Hanisch, 2025. Termination rights decide who is allowed to end the alliance, and when.

HOST A: And there are three levels.

HOST B: Level one: no termination provisions. You can only end it if the other side seriously breaks the contract. Otherwise, the most you can do is claim damages.

HOST A: So it's hard to leave.

HOST B: Very hard. Level two: conditional termination rights. You can end it, but only for specific reasons that both sides agreed on at the start.

HOST A: And level three?

HOST B: Unconditional termination rights. You can end it for any reason, or no reason at all, sometimes with a notice period.

HOST A: That sounds great for whoever has it.

HOST B: The slide showed a real contract. The company Grünenthal could end the agreement "for any or no reason", with one hundred and eighty days' written notice.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So why doesn't everyone just ask for that?

HOST B: Because it's a trade-off. On one side: protection and flexibility for you. On the other side: higher costs to negotiate the contract, and instability for the partnership.

HOST A: Because your partner never knows if you'll leave.

HOST B: Exactly. And the research found something interesting. In one thousand five hundred and seventy-six alliance contracts, when the client paid more money upfront, it was more likely to have an unconditional right to leave.

HOST A: [thoughtfully] That makes sense. If I pay a lot at the start, I want an exit door.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay, now networks. Companies don't just work in pairs.

HOST B: Right. Ritter and Gemünden, 2003, describe five levels, from small to big.

HOST A: Slowly.

HOST B: An interaction: one single exchange. A dyad: all the interactions between two actors over time.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: A portfolio: similar relationships a company has with other organisations, like all its suppliers. A net: all the relationships of one actor. And a network: the whole structure of an industry or market.

HOST A: Interaction, dyad, portfolio, net, network.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And why are networks growing?

HOST B: Digital technology. The lecture quotes Ocasio and colleagues: the old world was big, slow, vertically integrated corporations. Today's world is fast-moving, entrepreneurial tech companies with innovative business models.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Which brings us to platforms!

HOST B: Yes. Think Airbnb, Uber, booking dot com. The lecture asks: they create huge value, but how?

HOST A: And the answer is: they reduce transaction costs.

HOST B: In three ways. Let's go one by one.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: One.

HOST B: Match making. Connecting buyers and sellers who would otherwise struggle to find each other. With search, filters and recommendations.

HOST A: Like Airbnb showing me the right flat in Rome, in seconds.

HOST B: Exactly. This cuts search costs.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Two.

HOST B: Market making. The platform sets the rules, the prices and the standards, so deals happen smoothly. And it balances supply and demand.

HOST A: Like Uber raising prices when it rains, so more drivers come out.

HOST B: Great example. The lecture says this helps avoid adverse selection.

HOST A: Let's explain that word.

HOST B: Adverse selection is a problem before the deal. You can't tell the good offers from the bad ones, so bad ones creep in.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And three.

HOST B: Trust building. Ratings, reviews, verified profiles, guarantees, ways to settle disputes.

HOST A: So I can trust a total stranger's flat.

HOST B: Exactly. And this reduces moral hazard.

HOST A: Which is?

HOST B: A problem after the deal. Once you've committed, the other side might behave badly. Reviews keep everyone honest.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. Ratings and reviews on a platform. Which function is that, and which problem does it reduce?

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Trust building. It reduces moral hazard.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Then one-sided and two-sided platforms.

HOST B: A one-sided platform mostly offers its own content. Like Disney Plus: Disney makes the films, you watch them.

HOST A: And two-sided?

HOST B: It connects two separate groups. Like booking dot com: independent hotels on one side, travellers on the other. The hotels are called complementors.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now network effects. This is the heart of platforms.

HOST B: A network effect means a product becomes more valuable the more people use it.

HOST A: Like WhatsApp.

HOST B: Exactly. WhatsApp alone is useless. It gets better with every friend who joins. The lecture calls this demand-side economies of scale.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there are two types.

HOST B: Direct network effects: more users on the same side make it better. Like a messaging app.

HOST A: And indirect?

HOST B: More users on the other side make it better. More hosts on Airbnb make it better for guests. And more guests make it better for hosts.

HOST A: So direct is same side, indirect is the other side.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And now the maths. Don't panic, it's easy.

HOST B: [laughs] Promise. For direct network effects, how many possible connections are there between users?

HOST A: Go on.

HOST B: The formula is n times n minus one, divided by two. With eight users: eight times seven is fifty-six, divided by two is twenty-eight.

HOST A: Twenty-eight possible connections.

HOST B: For indirect effects, it's simpler: m times n. With four hosts and four guests, four times four is sixteen.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a formula for intermediaries too.

HOST B: Yes, from Baligh and Richartz, 1964. Without a middleman, every seller must deal with every buyer. That's m times n transactions.

HOST A: And with a middleman?

HOST B: Everyone only deals with the middleman. That's m plus n. So the saving is m times n, minus m plus n.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Let's try it, listener. Ten sellers and ten buyers. How many transactions does the platform save?

PAUSE: 10

HOST B: Without the platform: ten times ten, a hundred. With the platform: ten plus ten, twenty. So it saves eighty.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And this leads to "winner takes all".

HOST B: Yes. Network effects create a loop. More users attract more providers. More providers make it better for users. So the platform grows faster and faster.

HOST A: And competitors can't catch up.

HOST B: Right. It becomes hard to enter the market, and you get concentration and lock-in.

HOST A: Can anything stop it?

HOST B: Four counterforces. Multi-homing: using several platforms at once, like a driver on both Uber and Bolt. Compatibility between systems. Data portability: taking your data with you. And regulation.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: between market and hierarchy, there are hybrids. Market means cost of contracting, hybrid means cost of cooperation, hierarchy means cost of organizing.

HOST A: Two: partnerships go from consortium, to non-equity alliance, to equity alliance, to joint venture, to M and A. Three: alliances are held together by contracts, trust, and shared norms.

HOST B: And four: platforms reduce transaction costs through match making, market making and trust building, and grow through network effects.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Final quiz. In Williamson's picture, which form has the cost of cooperation?

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: The hybrid.

HOST A: What's the difference between direct and indirect network effects?

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Direct: value grows with more users on the same side. Indirect: value grows with more users on the other side.

HOST A: A messaging app has six users. How many direct connections are possible?

PAUSE: 10

HOST B: Six times five, divided by two. Fifteen.

HOST A: And name the four counterforces to winner takes all.

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Multi-homing, compatibility, data portability, and regulation.

HOST A: [warmly] Great work. Next time: strategy. Porter's five forces, and why some industries make so much more money than others.

HOST B: See you there!
