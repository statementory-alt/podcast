# Episode 6 · Innovation I · Lego, Ambidexterity and Growth

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome to episode six! Lecture five: innovation. How do firms create value?

HOST B: [cheerfully] And this one has Lego!

HOST A: [laughs] It does. But first a quick update to last week's model. The lecture starts with VRIO, from Barney in 1995.

HOST B: VRIO, not VRIN?

HOST A: Right. Valuable, rare, inimitable, and organised to capture value. So having a great resource isn't enough. The firm also has to be organised to actually use it.

HOST B: And absorptive capacity comes back.

HOST A: Yes. The ability to recognise the value of new outside information. It feeds into the firm's ability to organise and capture value.

HOST B: Okay, Lego. Tell me the story.

HOST A: Lego was founded in 1932 in Billund, Denmark, by a carpenter called Ole Kirk Kristiansen. Today it's one of the most successful toy companies in the world. In 2025, its operating profit was about twenty-two billion Danish crowns, around three point four billion dollars, up eighteen percent on the year before.

HOST B: But it wasn't always like that.

HOST A: No. Lego grew about fourteen percent a year for more than fifteen years, until 1993. Then sales slowed, and revenue fell in the nineties. By the late nineties, Lego had hit a plateau.

HOST B: So what did they do?

HOST A: The leadership believed building blocks were outdated, and that kids wanted digital toys. So until about 2002, they tried to reinvent the brand. They experimented with lots of things beyond the classic bricks.

HOST B: Like what?

HOST A: Things like theme parks, video games, clothing, and licences for brands like Harry Potter and Star Wars.

HOST B: And did it work?

HOST A: [seriously] It nearly killed the company. Lego made big losses in several years between 2000 and 2004. In 2004, it lost almost two billion Danish crowns. It was close to bankruptcy.

HOST B: [surprised] Wow. So why did all that innovation fail?

HOST A: That's the discussion question from the lecture. The short answer: they explored too much, too far from what they were good at, and neglected their core business.

HOST B: And then the comeback.

HOST A: Yes. From 2007 on, revenue climbed from about eight billion Danish crowns to over eighty-three billion in 2025, with profits over sixteen billion. A huge turnaround.

HOST B: Quick check for the listener. In which year was Lego founded, and where?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: 1932, in Billund, Denmark.

HOST B: Okay, the theory behind this. Ambidexterity.

HOST A: Yes. Ambidexterity literally means being able to use both hands equally. In business, it means doing two things at the same time: exploitation and exploration.

HOST B: Explain both.

HOST A: Exploitation: building on what you already have. Incremental innovation, making things a bit better. Exploration: discovering new things. Radical innovation.

HOST B: And the danger is doing only one.

HOST A: Exactly. Only exploitation, and you get stuck, like Lego's plateau. Only exploration, and you lose your core, like Lego's near-bankruptcy. This idea comes from James March, in 1991.

HOST B: And it links to the St. Gallen model.

HOST A: Right. In the model, it's the core tension between the two development modes: optimization and renewal.

HOST B: There are three types of ambidexterity.

HOST A: Yes, from O'Reilly and Tushman. Sequential: the whole organisation switches over time, with periods of exploration and long stretches of exploitation. It separates them in time.

HOST B: Second?

HOST A: Structural: the firm separates exploration and exploitation into different units or departments, each with its own culture and structures.

HOST B: And third?

HOST A: Contextual: the firm builds a culture where each employee decides for themselves how to split their time between exploring and exploiting in their daily work.

HOST B: Pause and answer. A bank creates a separate digital lab with its own team and culture, while the main bank keeps running as usual. Which type of ambidexterity?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Structural ambidexterity. The two activities are in separate units.

HOST B: And then multidexterity.

HOST A: Yes, from Heracleous and Reeves, 2025. In the digital age, it's not just two hands. You combine exploitation, exploration, and digital transformation, meaning integrating digital technologies. Their examples were Lego and Netflix.

HOST B: There was a funny animal picture in the break.

HOST A: [laughs] An optical illusion, "which animal do you see?". A nice reminder that the same situation can look completely different depending on how you look at it.

HOST B: Okay. What can firms do to grow when just exploiting the core isn't enough?

HOST A: The Ansoff matrix. It has two questions: are the products existing or new, and are the markets existing or new? That gives four growth options.

HOST B: Go through them.

HOST A: Existing products in existing markets: market penetration. Selling more of what you already sell to the same customers. New products in existing markets: product development. Existing products in new markets: market development, like going abroad. And new products in new markets: diversification.

HOST B: Quick quiz. On, the Swiss running shoe company, starts selling its shoes in Australia. Which Ansoff strategy?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Market development. An existing product in a new market.

HOST B: And diversification is risky, right?

HOST A: It's a trade-off. Palich and colleagues, in 2000, found a curved relationship. Performance goes up when a firm moves from a single business to related diversification. But it goes down again when it moves to unrelated diversification.

HOST B: So like an upside-down U.

HOST A: Exactly. A bit of related diversification is good. Diversifying into unrelated businesses tends to hurt.

HOST B: Next: where do new ideas come from?

HOST A: Open innovation, from Henry Chesbrough, 2003. The idea is that firms shouldn't only rely on their own research. The idea-generation process opens up beyond the firm's boundaries.

HOST B: In which directions?

HOST A: Two. Outside-in: knowledge and ideas come into the firm from outside. That's external knowledge integration. And inside-out: knowledge and ideas from inside the firm flow out, for example by licensing them. That's external knowledge utilisation.

HOST B: And Lego does this!

HOST A: Yes. With Lego Ideas, fans submit their own set designs. If enough people support an idea, Lego can turn it into a real product. That brings in ideas from outside and taps into a new audience, including adult fans, which drives growth.

HOST B: Pause and answer. Lego Ideas: outside-in or inside-out?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Outside-in. Ideas from fans come into the firm.

HOST B: And the last part: business model innovation.

HOST A: Right. Besides new products and new markets, you can innovate the business model itself. The example on the slide was IKEA: flat-pack packaging, modular design, customers assembling the furniture themselves, self-service stores, and furniture as fashion.

HOST B: [laughs] So IKEA made us do half the work, and we love it.

HOST A: [laughs] Exactly. And the tool for this is the Business Model Canvas, from Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010. It shows that you can innovate a business model in many different places.

HOST B: The textbook also defines business models, right?

HOST A: Yes. It says a business model answers three core questions, just like strategy: how is value created, for whom, and how does the firm capture that value?

HOST B: And the punchline?

HOST A: Make the most of what you have and create something new, at the same time. Ambidexterity couples exploitation and exploration. The Ansoff matrix and business model innovation enable renewal. And open innovation pushes firms to engage with the outside world.

HOST B: Final quiz! What does VRIO stand for?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Valuable, rare, inimitable, and organised to capture value.

HOST B: Name the three types of ambidexterity.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Sequential, structural, and contextual.

HOST B: New products in new markets: which Ansoff option?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Diversification.

HOST B: According to Palich and colleagues, which kind of diversification performs best?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Related diversification. Unrelated diversification performs worse.

HOST B: [warmly] And that's the series so far: value creation, why firms exist, alliances and platforms, two lectures on strategy, and innovation.

HOST A: Good luck with your touchpoints, and see you next time!
