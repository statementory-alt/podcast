# Episode 6 · Innovation I · Lego, Ambidexterity and Growth

HOST B: [curious] Here's a story. A toy company grows fast for over fifteen years. Then sales slow down. The managers panic, and decide: kids don't want our old product anymore. Let's try everything new.

HOST A: Sounds reasonable.

HOST B: And a few years later, the company is close to bankruptcy.

HOST A: [surprised] What? Which company?

HOST B: Lego.

HOST A: No way.

HOST B: And today it's one of the most successful toy companies in the world. So what went wrong, and what went right? That's today's episode.

HOST A: Welcome back to the Business Administration study podcast. Episode six: innovation.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Here's the plan. First, a small update to last week's VRIN model. Then the Lego story. Then the big idea behind it: ambidexterity. Then the Ansoff matrix, four ways to grow. Then open innovation. And finally, how to innovate a whole business model.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So, the update.

HOST B: Last episode we had VRIN: valuable, rare, inimitable, non-substitutable. In 1995, Barney updated it to VRIO.

HOST A: What's the O?

HOST B: Organised. Organised to capture value. So having a great resource isn't enough. The company also has to be set up to actually use it.

HOST A: Like having a great player on your team, but never passing him the ball.

HOST B: [laughs] Exactly. And absorptive capacity, the ability to recognise valuable new information from outside, helps the company organise to capture value.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay. Now Lego. Let's go slowly.

HOST B: Lego was founded in 1932, in Billund, Denmark, by a carpenter called Ole Kirk Kristiansen.

HOST A: A carpenter. So it started with wooden toys.

HOST B: Right. Today it's huge. In 2025, its operating profit was about twenty-two billion Danish crowns. That's about three point four billion dollars, and eighteen percent more than the year before.

HOST A: Okay. So where's the drama?

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Until 1993, Lego grew about fourteen percent a year, for more than fifteen years.

HOST A: That's amazing growth.

HOST B: But then sales slowed down, and in the nineties revenue actually fell. Lego hit a plateau.

HOST A: And management reacted.

HOST B: Yes. The leadership believed that building blocks were outdated, and that kids wanted digital toys. So until about 2002, they tried to reinvent the brand.

HOST A: How?

HOST B: They experimented with lots of things beyond the classic bricks. The slides show licences, for example for Harry Potter and Star Wars. Lego also moved into areas like video games, theme parks and clothing.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And?

HOST B: [seriously] It almost killed the company. Lego lost money in several years. In 2003, it lost about one billion Danish crowns. In 2004, almost two billion.

HOST A: [surprised] Wow. So close to bankruptcy.

HOST B: Very close.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: So why did all that innovation fail?

HOST B: That's the discussion question from the lecture. Here's one way to see it. Lego tried too many new things, too far from what it was good at. And it neglected its core business: the bricks.

HOST A: So innovation itself wasn't the problem. Losing the core was.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And then the comeback.

HOST B: Yes. From 2007 on, revenue grew from about eight billion Danish crowns to more than eighty-three billion in 2025. And profit grew from about one billion to almost seventeen billion.

HOST A: That's a tenfold increase.

HOST B: One of the great turnarounds in business.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. When and where was Lego founded?

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: In 1932, in Billund, Denmark.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Now the theory behind the story. Ambidexterity.

HOST B: Ambidexterity literally means using both hands equally well. In business, it means doing two very different things at the same time.

HOST A: Which two?

HOST B: Exploitation and exploration. Let's take them one at a time.

HOST A: Exploitation first.

HOST B: Exploitation means building on what you already have. Small, step-by-step improvements. Incremental innovation.

HOST A: Like making the bricks a bit better every year.

HOST B: Exactly. And exploration means discovering something new. Big leaps. Radical innovation.

HOST A: Like going into video games.

HOST B: Right.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And the danger is doing only one of them.

HOST B: Exactly. If you only exploit, you get stuck. That was Lego's plateau. If you only explore, you lose your core. That was Lego's near-bankruptcy.

HOST A: So you need both.

HOST B: Both, at the same time. This idea goes back to James March, in 1991.

HOST A: And it connects to the St. Gallen model, right?

HOST B: Yes. Remember the two development modes: optimization and renewal. Ambidexterity is about handling the tension between them.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And there are three ways to do it.

HOST B: Yes, from O'Reilly and Tushman. Three types of ambidexterity.

HOST A: Slowly.

HOST B: One: sequential ambidexterity. The whole company switches over time. A phase of exploring, then a long phase of exploiting, then exploring again.

HOST A: So it separates them in time.

HOST B: Exactly. Two: structural ambidexterity. The company separates them into different units. One department explores, another exploits, each with its own culture and rules.

HOST A: Like a separate innovation lab.

HOST B: Exactly. And three: contextual ambidexterity. The company creates a culture where every employee decides for themselves how to split their time between exploring and exploiting, in their daily work.

HOST A: So in time, in separate units, or inside each person.

HOST B: Perfect summary.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Your turn, listener. A bank creates a separate digital lab, with its own team and culture, while the main bank keeps running as usual. Which type of ambidexterity?

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Structural ambidexterity. The two activities are in separate units.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a new twist: multidexterity.

HOST B: Yes, from Heracleous and Reeves, 2025. In the digital age, two hands aren't enough. You combine exploitation, exploration, and digital transformation, meaning bringing digital technology into everything. Their examples were Lego and Netflix.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay. Next question. How can a company grow, when just doing more of the same isn't enough?

HOST B: The Ansoff matrix. It's simple. You ask two questions. Are the products existing or new? And are the markets existing or new?

HOST A: Two questions, two answers each. So four options.

HOST B: Exactly. Let's go one by one.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Existing products, existing markets.

HOST B: Market penetration. Selling more of what you already sell, to the same kind of customers.

HOST A: Like a bakery running a promotion to sell more bread to its neighbours.

HOST B: Exactly.

HOST A: New products, existing markets.

HOST B: Product development. The bakery starts selling cakes to the same neighbours.

HOST A: Existing products, new markets.

HOST B: Market development. The bakery opens a shop in another city, or sells its bread abroad.

HOST A: And new products in new markets.

HOST B: Diversification. The bakery starts selling bicycles to tourists.

HOST A: [laughs] That sounds risky.

HOST B: It is. We'll come back to that.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Your turn. On, the Swiss running shoe company, starts selling its existing shoes in Australia. Which Ansoff strategy?

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Market development. An existing product in a new market.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So, is diversification good or bad?

HOST B: Research by Palich and colleagues, in 2000, gives a nuanced answer. Think of a hill.

HOST A: A hill?

HOST B: Yes. If a company with a single business moves into related businesses, performance goes up. That's the way up the hill.

HOST A: Related, meaning close to what it already does.

HOST B: Exactly. But if it moves on into unrelated businesses, performance goes down again. That's the way down the other side.

HOST A: So a bit of related diversification is good. Unrelated diversification tends to hurt.

HOST B: Exactly. The bakery selling cakes: good. The bakery selling bicycles: probably bad.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Next: where do new ideas come from?

HOST B: Open innovation. The idea comes from Henry Chesbrough, in 2003. Companies shouldn't rely only on their own research department. They should open up to ideas from outside.

HOST A: In which direction?

HOST B: Two directions. Outside-in: ideas and knowledge come into the company from outside. Like customers or universities sharing ideas.

HOST A: And inside-out?

HOST B: Ideas from inside the company go out. For example, licensing a technology to other companies that can use it better.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And Lego does this too.

HOST B: Yes, with Lego Ideas. Fans design their own sets and submit them online. If enough people support an idea, Lego can turn it into a real product.

HOST A: That's clever.

HOST B: And it brings in new ideas, and reaches new audiences, which drives growth.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Quick check. Lego Ideas: outside-in or inside-out?

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: Outside-in. Ideas from fans come into the company.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And the last topic: business model innovation.

HOST B: Yes. Besides new products and new markets, a company can change the whole way it does business.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: The slide shows a famous furniture model. Flat-pack boxes. Modular designs. Customers assembling the furniture themselves. Self-service warehouses. And furniture as fashion, cheap enough to change often.

HOST A: [laughs] That's IKEA. They made us do half the work, and we love it.

HOST B: [laughs] Exactly. And the tool for thinking about this is the Business Model Canvas, from Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010. It shows that there are many different places in a business model where you can innovate.

HOST A: And the course book defines a business model too.

HOST B: Yes. It answers three questions. How is value created? For whom? And how does the company capture part of that value?

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: VRIO adds "organised". A great resource only helps if the company is set up to use it. Two: Lego shows that you need both exploitation and exploration. That's ambidexterity.

HOST A: Three: there are three types of ambidexterity: sequential, structural, and contextual. Four: the Ansoff matrix gives four ways to grow, and related diversification beats unrelated. And five: open innovation brings ideas in and out, and business models can be innovated too.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Diversification.

HOST B: And according to Palich and colleagues, which kind of diversification performs best?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Related diversification. Unrelated diversification tends to perform worse.
