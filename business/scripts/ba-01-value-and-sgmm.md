# Episode 1 · Value Creation and the St. Gallen Management Model

HOST A: [warmly] Hey, welcome to the Business Administration study podcast! This is episode one.

HOST B: [cheerfully] And today we're starting at the very beginning. What is business administration actually about? And what's this famous St. Gallen model everyone keeps talking about?

HOST A: Exactly. Quick tip before we start: when you hear a question, try to answer it out loud. We'll leave you a few seconds of silence.

HOST B: Love it. So, first things first. How does this course work?

HOST A: The lecture says the course has four pillars. Lectures, for the terms and the theories. Self-study, to go deeper. Exercise groups, where you apply things to real cases. And a running case, where you practise teamwork and solving complex problems.

HOST B: And the running case is a real company, right?

HOST A: Yes. It's On, the Swiss running shoe company. You work on it in chapters, called touchpoints, and they're due on Tuesdays at 23:59 on Canvas.

HOST B: And the content is organised around five big questions.

HOST A: Right. Organizing: why do firms exist? Strategy: how do firms capture value? Innovation: how do firms create value? Marketing: how do firms deliver value? And supply chain: how do firms orchestrate value? Plus integration at the end: how do firms build value systems?

HOST B: [curious] Okay. And the exam?

HOST A: [seriously] Write this down. Digital, on campus, on the twenty-sixth of January. It counts one hundred percent. It's one hundred and eighty minutes, for one hundred and eighty points. So, roughly a point a minute.

HOST B: [laughs] That's actually a nice way to remember it.

HOST A: It's built around a case, an unfamiliar company. There are five tasks that follow the content blocks, and the questions are closed-ended, focused on the core learning objectives.

HOST B: So the whole course trains you to look at a company you've never seen and analyse it quickly.

HOST A: That's exactly it. And that's why today's tools matter so much.

HOST B: Okay. So what does a business actually do?

HOST A: It creates value. The lecture describes the value creation process. You start with resources: labour, knowledge, capital and materials. Then a transformation process turns them into an output: products, services, or solutions. And the customer perceives some value in that.

HOST B: And there's a definition of value we need to know.

HOST A: Yes, learn this one by heart. [slowly] Value is the benefit that customers attribute to a product, minus the costs of all the steps required to produce it.

HOST B: So it's not just "people like it". It's how much they like it, minus what it costs to make.

HOST A: Exactly. And this value creation process actually defines the sub-disciplines of business administration.

HOST B: Which are?

HOST A: Research and development. Strategy. Marketing. Operations. Accounting and control. And organizational behaviour. All of them sit around value creation.

HOST B: There was a car example too.

HOST A: Yes. The car industry shows how complex value creation gets. Take Mercedes-Benz as the carmaker, the OEM. It gets steel from ArcelorMittal, tyres from Continental, modules from Bosch. Those suppliers have their own suppliers. Then logistics firms like DB Schenker move everything around, and dealers, like the slide's example, the Emil Frey auto dealer, sell the cars to customers.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] So one car is really a whole chain of companies working together.

HOST A: Exactly. And the lecture adds a historical point. Trade has existed for thousands of years, think of the Fugger family in the Middle Ages. But the scientific study of business is only about a century old. It started with two questions: how can companies operate more productively, and why are some companies more successful than others?

HOST B: Now the big one. The St. Gallen Management Model.

HOST A: Yes, the SGMM. The course uses version five. And the week one reading says the lecture, the exercises, and the exam all use the same figure. So it's really worth knowing.

HOST B: How do I picture it?

HOST A: Think of three layers, like an onion. On the outside, the environmental spheres. In the middle, a ring of stakeholders. And inside, the firm itself.

HOST B: Start with the outside.

HOST A: The environmental spheres are areas where things change, but the firm doesn't control them. It just feels them. In this course there are five: society, nature, technology, politics, and law.

HOST B: And markets?

HOST A: [excited] Great question, because that's a classic trap. Markets, capital and competition are not spheres in this model. They show up as stakeholders, as actors.

HOST B: Okay, so the middle ring. The stakeholders.

HOST A: Stakeholders are actors who take part in the firm's value creation, or are affected by it. Each one gives the firm something and expects something back. And the model even says what they expect.

HOST B: Go through them.

HOST A: Investors expect interest, or returns. Customers expect value, or performance. Employees expect pay. The state expects taxes. Suppliers expect orders. Competitors claim market share. And the media expect information.

HOST B: [playfully] Okay, let me try this on our listener. In the St. Gallen model, what does the state expect from the firm?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Taxes. And in return, the state gives the firm a legal framework.

HOST B: And the inside of the firm?

HOST A: Inside, you have value creation as processes. There are three kinds. Management processes, which steer the firm. Business processes, the core work, like making and selling. And support processes, like finance or HR.

HOST B: There's more on the inside, right?

HOST A: Yes. Above the processes are the things that give the whole firm its order: governance, strategy, structure, and culture. And then there are the two development modes: optimization and renewal.

HOST B: What's the difference?

HOST A: Optimization means improving what already exists, step by step. Renewal means changing something fundamental, challenging the status quo.

HOST B: Quick check. What are the three types of processes in the St. Gallen model?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Management processes, business processes, and support processes.

HOST B: Okay, now the week one reading. It's called "The firm in its environment". And I heard it's really practical.

HOST A: It is. It follows one example. Imagine a regional energy company called Regionalwerk. Four hundred employees, a power grid, eighty thousand households as customers, and it's owned by twelve local municipalities.

HOST B: And one morning...

HOST A: [dramatically] One morning, the news says home batteries will be half as expensive in two years.

HOST B: Uh oh. Why is that bad for them?

HOST A: Let's work it out with the reading's questions. Question one: where in our environment is something changing?

HOST B: Technology!

HOST A: Exactly. That's the sphere. And the rule of thumb is: for every change, first name the sphere where it starts.

HOST B: But a sphere can't call you on the phone.

HOST A: [laughs] That's literally the reading's point. Question two: who brings the change to the firm? A sphere doesn't write letters. An actor has to pick it up. Here, households who use batteries and buy less electricity. The municipalities, worried about their dividend. And the regulator who approves grid prices.

HOST B: And there's a little test for this.

HOST A: The letter test! You can write a letter to an actor. You can't write a letter to "regulation". The sphere is law, the actor is the regulator. You can't write a letter to "the market" either. The actor is the customers, or a competitor.

HOST B: [laughs] I love that. And what about nature? Nature can't complain.

HOST A: Right, nature can't make claims itself. Environmental groups or authorities speak for it.

HOST B: So to locate a challenge, I need three things.

HOST A: Exactly. The sphere, the actor, and the place inside the firm where it hits. For cheap batteries: the sphere is technology. The actor is households as customers. And inside, it hits strategy, in the mode of renewal, because the business model itself has to change.

HOST B: Your turn, listener. A new law bans fossil heating. What's the sphere, and who is the actor?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: The sphere is law, or politics. The actor is the state or the authority that enforces it. "The law" itself is not an actor.

HOST B: Okay, question three. How does the change get inside the firm?

HOST A: With a causal chain. It has three links. First, the trigger: what sets it off. Second, the effect: what concretely changes inside the firm, like revenues, costs or processes. And third, what it forces: what the firm now has to do.

HOST B: For Regionalwerk?

HOST A: Batteries get half as cheap. Households store their own power, so grid revenues fall by about a fifth. So Regionalwerk has to introduce a new tariff model with a basic fee.

HOST B: And there's a classic bad example.

HOST A: [laughs] Yes. "Competition is tough, so profits fall, so the firm is worse off." That's not a chain. The second link just repeats the first, and the last one is a state, not an action. Rule: every link must say something new, and the last one must be an action.

HOST B: And if there are several challenges, how do I pick the biggest one?

HOST A: You name your criterion first. The reading gives four: existential relevance, time horizon, reversibility, or room for manoeuvre. A choice without a criterion is just an opinion.

HOST B: Ooh, I like that line.

HOST A: Question four: whose expectation counts? Freeman defines stakeholders as "any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives".

HOST B: Affect, or be affected. Two different things.

HOST A: Exactly, and the reading turns them into two questions. Influence: what can this group give us, or take away from us? Capital, permits, orders, work. That's the strategic view, from Freeman. And affectedness: does this group bear the consequences of what we do, whether or not it can fight back? That's the ethical view, from Ulrich.

HOST B: Example?

HOST A: The regulator has high influence, it approves the prices, but it isn't affected by them. Residents next to a new battery plant have little influence, but they live with the noise. And if you ignore them, their affectedness can turn into influence later, through the media or politics.

HOST B: Quick one. What are the two questions you ask about every stakeholder?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: What can it withdraw from us? That's influence. And does it bear the consequences? That's affectedness.

HOST B: And the last question. What must the firm do?

HOST A: Four steps, and all four must appear in your answer. Choose a stakeholder and say why. Classify it on influence and affectedness. Name its expectation, concretely, not just "satisfaction". And state the consequence for the business.

HOST B: For the regulator?

HOST A: Chosen because it decides if the new tariffs happen at all. High influence, low affectedness. It expects transparent cost accounting before every price change. Consequence: Regionalwerk can't finance the change with sudden price jumps. It has to plan and pre-finance the investments over several years.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] And "the regulator is important, we must keep a good relationship" is...

HOST A: [laughs] None of the four steps. Too vague.

HOST B: The textbook adds a little more to this, right?

HOST A: Yes, the course book is "An Introduction to Management Studies" by Bieger and colleagues. It says the SGMM has stayed built on three elements over the years: environment, organization, and management. And it adds the idea of interaction issues: the topics a firm negotiates with its stakeholders, like resources, norms and values, or concerns and interests. Job security with employees is a classic example.

HOST B: Final quiz! Answer before we do. What's the definition of value?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: The benefit customers attribute to a product, minus the costs of all the steps needed to produce it.

HOST B: Name the five environmental spheres in this course.

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Society, nature, technology, politics, and law.

HOST B: What are the three links of a causal chain?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Trigger, effect, and what it forces. And the last one has to be an action.

HOST B: And is "the market" an environmental sphere?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: No! Markets, capital and competition show up as stakeholders, like customers or competitors.

HOST B: [warmly] Nice work. Next episode: the big question of lecture one. Why do firms exist at all? We'll talk about Adam Smith, Coase, make or buy, and why Tesla builds its own batteries.

HOST A: See you there!
