HOST A: So what the course is really training is this: someone hands you an unfamiliar company, and you have to analyse it quickly and correctly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Let's get into the content. What does a business actually do?

HOST B: The lecture's answer is: it creates value.

HOST A: That sounds a bit like a slogan.

HOST B: [laughs] It does. So let's make it concrete. Think of a bakery.

HOST A: Okay, I'm hungry already.

HOST B: The baker starts with resources. Flour, an oven, money, and their own skill and experience.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: The lecture says there are four kinds of resources: labour, knowledge, capital, and materials.

HOST A: So in the bakery: the baker's work is labour. Knowing the recipe is knowledge. The oven and the money are capital. And the flour is materials.

HOST B: Perfect. Then comes the transformation process. Mixing, kneading, baking.

HOST A: And out comes bread.

HOST B: Out comes an output. The lecture says the output can be a product, a service, or a solution. And the customer sees some value in it.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So what exactly is "value"?

HOST B: Here's the definition from the lecture. It's one to learn by heart. [slowly] Value is the benefit that customers attribute to a product, minus the costs of all the steps required to produce it.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let me say that back in my own words. Value is how much the customer gets out of it, minus everything it cost to make it.

HOST B: Exactly. So if people love your bread, but it costs you a fortune to make, you're not creating much value.

HOST A: And if it's cheap to make, but nobody wants it...

HOST B: Also not much value. You need both: real benefit for the customer, and reasonable costs.

HOST A: Got it.

HOST B: Quick check. What are the four kinds of resources a firm transforms?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Labour, knowledge, capital, and materials.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Now, here's a neat idea from the lecture. This value creation process is actually what defines the different fields of business administration.

HOST A: How so?

HOST B: Each field looks at one part of creating value. Research and development: inventing new products. Strategy: deciding where and how to compete. Marketing: understanding and reaching customers. Operations: actually producing things. Accounting and control: tracking money and results. And organizational behaviour: how people work together.

HOST A: So all those subjects you see in a business degree are really just different angles on the same question: how do we create value?

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And in real life, value creation is rarely done by one company alone, right?

HOST B: Right. The lecture uses the car industry. Take Mercedes-Benz. It doesn't make a car from scratch.

HOST A: Where does the steel come from?

HOST B: From a steel company, like ArcelorMittal. The tyres come from Continental. Electronic modules come from Bosch. And each of those has its own suppliers.

HOST A: And then someone has to move all of it around.

HOST B: Logistics companies, like DB Schenker, and shipping lines. And at the end, a car dealer, like Emil Frey, sells the car to you.

HOST A: [thoughtfully] So one car is really a long chain of companies, each adding a bit of value.

HOST B: That's exactly the picture.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: One more thing from the lecture, before the model. Business itself is ancient, but studying it scientifically is quite new.

HOST B: Yes. People have traded for thousands of years. The slide mentions the Fugger family, rich merchants in the Middle Ages. But the scientific study of business only started a bit over a hundred years ago.

HOST A: And it started with two questions.

HOST B: How can companies work more productively? And why are some companies more successful than others?

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Now the big one. The St. Gallen Management Model.

HOST B: The SGMM. And this matters a lot, because the reading says the lectures, the exercises, and the exam all use the same figure.

HOST A: So we really need to know it. How should I picture it?

HOST B: Picture three circles, one inside the other. Like a target. On the outside, the environment. In the middle ring, the stakeholders. And in the centre, the company itself.

HOST A: Outside, middle, centre. Let's take them one at a time.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: The outer layer: the environmental spheres.

HOST A: What's a sphere?

HOST B: A sphere is an area where things change, but the company doesn't control them. The company just feels the effects.

HOST A: Like the weather.

HOST B: [laughs] Good comparison. In this course, there are five spheres: society, nature, technology, politics, and law.

HOST A: Society, nature, technology, politics, law.

HOST B: And there's one trap the reading warns about. Markets, capital and competition are not spheres in this model.

HOST A: Wait, really? The market isn't part of the environment?

HOST B: Not as a sphere. In this model, those show up as actors in the middle ring. Customers, investors, competitors.

HOST A: Okay, that's a classic exam trap. Good to know.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So, the middle ring. The stakeholders.

HOST B: Stakeholders are actors: people or organisations who take part in what the company does, or who are affected by it.

HOST A: And the key idea is a kind of exchange, right?

HOST B: Exactly. Each stakeholder gives the company something, and expects something back.

HOST A: Can we go through them?

HOST B: Sure. Investors give money, and they expect returns. Customers expect value, they want a good product. Employees give their work, and they expect pay.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: The state gives a legal framework, and it expects taxes. Suppliers expect orders. Competitors fight for market share. And the media expect information.

HOST A: [laughs] So everyone wants something.

HOST B: Everyone wants something. And management's job is to keep all these groups on board at the same time.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Let me test our listener. In the St. Gallen model, what does the state expect from the company?

PAUSE: 5

HOST B: Taxes. And in return, the state provides the legal framework.

HOST A: And is "the market" a sphere?

PAUSE: 5

HOST B: No. Markets show up as actors, like customers or competitors.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay, the centre. The company itself.

HOST B: In the centre, you find the processes. There are three kinds.

HOST A: Go slowly.

HOST B: First, management processes. These steer the company. Planning, deciding, leading.

HOST A: Like the brain.

HOST B: Nice. Second, business processes. That's the core work: making the product and selling it.

HOST A: In the bakery, baking and selling bread.

HOST B: Exactly. Third, support processes. Things that help the core work, like finance or HR.

HOST A: Management, business, support.

HOST B: Then, above the processes, there are four things that give the company its order: governance, strategy, structure, and culture.

HOST A: And there's one last part.

HOST B: The development modes. A company changes in one of two ways. Optimization: improving what already exists, step by step. Or renewal: changing something fundamental.

HOST A: So optimization is baking the same bread a bit better every year.

HOST B: And renewal is deciding to become a café, or to sell online.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Quick recap of the whole model before we move on.

HOST B: Outside: five spheres the company doesn't control. Society, nature, technology, politics, law. In the middle: stakeholders, who each give something and expect something. In the centre: three kinds of processes, plus governance, strategy, structure and culture, and two ways to change: optimization and renewal.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay. Now let's go back to our electricity company. This is the week one reading, and it's very practical.

HOST B: The reading calls the company Regionalwerk. Four hundred employees, a power grid, eighty thousand households as customers. And it's owned by twelve local municipalities.

HOST A: And the news: batteries will be half as expensive in two years.

HOST B: The reading walks through five questions. We'll take them one at a time.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question one. Where in our environment is something changing?

HOST B: What do you think?

HOST A: Batteries... that's technology.

HOST B: Exactly. The change starts in the sphere of technology. And the first rule of thumb from the reading is: for every change, first name the sphere where it starts.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question two. Who brings the change to the company?

HOST B: This is the clever part. A sphere can't knock on your door. Technology doesn't send you an angry email.

HOST A: [laughs] True.

HOST B: The change only becomes a problem when some actor picks it up. For Regionalwerk: households who buy batteries, store their own electricity, and buy less from the company.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: Also the municipalities who own it, and who worry about their dividend. And the regulator who approves electricity prices.

HOST A: And there's a simple test for this, right?

HOST B: The letter test. You can write a letter to an actor. You can't write a letter to a sphere.

HOST A: So I can write to the regulator, but not to "regulation".

HOST B: Exactly. "Regulation" is the sphere of law. The regulator is the actor. And you can't write to "the market" either. You'd write to customers, or to a competitor.

HOST A: And nature? Nature can't complain.

HOST B: Right. Nature can't make claims itself. Environmental groups or authorities speak for it.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So, to fully locate a challenge, I need three things.

HOST B: Three things. The sphere, the actor, and the place inside the company where it hits.

HOST A: And for the batteries?

HOST B: Sphere: technology. Actor: households, as customers. And inside the company, it hits the strategy, because the whole business model has to change. So the development mode is renewal, not just optimization.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Your turn, listener. A new law bans oil heating in new houses. What's the sphere, and who's the actor?

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: The sphere is law, or politics. The actor is the authority that enforces the law. "The law" itself is not an actor.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question three. How does the change actually get inside the company?

HOST B: With a causal chain. It has three links. Let's take them slowly.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: Link one: the trigger. What sets everything off.

HOST A: Batteries get cheaper.

HOST B: Link two: the effect. What concretely changes inside the company. Revenues, costs, processes.

HOST A: Households store their own power, so Regionalwerk sells less. The reading says grid revenues fall by about a fifth.

HOST B: And link three: what it forces. What the company now has to do.

HOST A: Regionalwerk has to change its prices. The reading says: introduce a tariff model with a basic fee.

HOST B: Perfect. Trigger, effect, what it forces.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a classic mistake here, right?

HOST B: Yes. Listen to this: "Competition is tough, so profits fall, so the company is worse off."

HOST A: That sounds... fine?

HOST B: It sounds fine, but it's not a chain. The second link just repeats the first in other words. And the last link is a state, "worse off", not an action.

HOST A: So the rule is: every link must add something new, and the last link must be something the company does.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And if there are several challenges, how do I choose the biggest one?

HOST B: You name your criterion first. The reading gives four options: existential relevance, meaning does it threaten survival. Time horizon, meaning how soon it hits. Reversibility, meaning can you undo it. And room for manoeuvre, meaning how much freedom you have left.

HOST A: And without a criterion?

HOST B: The reading puts it nicely: a choice without a criterion is just an opinion.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Question four. Whose expectation counts?

HOST B: Here, the reading uses Freeman's famous definition. A stakeholder is [slowly] "any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives".

HOST A: "Can affect, or is affected." Those sound like two different things.

HOST B: They are! And the reading turns them into two questions.

HOST A: Go.

HOST B: Question one: influence. What can this group give us, or take away from us? Money, permits, orders, work. That's the strategic view, from Freeman.

HOST A: And question two?

HOST B: Affectedness. Does this group live with the consequences of what we do, even if it can't fight back? That's the ethical view, from Ulrich.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Can you give an example where they're different?

HOST B: Sure. The regulator has huge influence. It approves the prices. But it's not affected by them.

HOST A: And the opposite?

HOST B: The people living next to a new battery plant. They have little influence, but they live with the noise and the land use.

HOST A: So if I only look at influence, I forget the neighbours.

HOST B: And later, their anger can turn into influence, through the media or politics.

HOST A: And if I only look at affectedness?

HOST B: Then everyone is important, and you can't decide anything. That's why you need both questions.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. What two questions do you ask about every stakeholder?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: What can it take away from us? That's influence. And does it bear the consequences? That's affectedness.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Last question. What must the company actually do?

HOST B: Four steps. And the reading says all four must appear in your answer.

HOST A: Slowly, please.

HOST B: Step one: choose a stakeholder, and say why this one.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: Step two: classify it. How much influence, how much affectedness, using facts from the case.

HOST A: Step three?

HOST B: Its expectation. And it has to be concrete. Not "they want to be satisfied".

HOST A: [laughs] Everyone wants to be satisfied.

HOST B: Exactly, that's why it's useless. And step four: the consequence. What the company must now do, or can no longer do, for its business.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Let's do it with the regulator.

HOST B: Step one: we choose the regulator, because it decides whether the new prices happen at all. Step two: high influence, low affectedness. Step three: it expects transparent cost accounting before every price change.

HOST A: And step four?

HOST B: Regionalwerk can't pay for the change with sudden price jumps. It has to plan and pre-finance its investments over several years.

HOST A: And a bad answer would be...

HOST B: "The regulator is important, so we must keep a good relationship." That's none of the four steps.

HOST A: Four: a causal chain goes trigger, effect, what it forces, and it ends with an action. And five: for each stakeholder, ask about influence and affectedness, then go choose, classify, expectation, consequence.
