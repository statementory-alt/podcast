# Episode 2 · Gains from Trade

HOST A: [curious] Here's a puzzle. Switzerland is more productive than many developing countries at almost everything. So why does Switzerland trade with them at all? Why not just make everything itself?

HOST B: Hmm. Because it's cheaper to import some things?

HOST A: Getting warmer. And the real answer is one of the most surprising ideas in all of economics.

HOST B: Welcome back to the Economics A study podcast. Episode two: gains from trade.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Here's the plan. First, the key words: autarky, absolute advantage, and comparative advantage. Then we calculate opportunity costs, slowly, with a simple example. Then we see why both sides gain from trade. And at the end, the darker side: who loses, and what risks come with specialising.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Let's start with autarky.

HOST A: Autarky means no trade at all. Everyone produces everything they consume themselves.

HOST B: Like living alone on an island.

HOST A: Exactly. Robinson Crusoe. You eat only what you catch, you wear only what you make.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And now the two kinds of advantage.

HOST A: First, absolute advantage. You have an absolute advantage in a good if you can produce more of it than the other person, with the same resources.

HOST B: So simply: you're better at it.

HOST A: Exactly. If Anna can make thirty pizzas a day and Ben only twenty, Anna has the absolute advantage in pizza.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And comparative advantage?

HOST A: You have a comparative advantage in a good if your opportunity cost of producing it is lower than the other person's.

HOST B: So it's not about being better. It's about giving up less.

HOST A: Exactly. And that's the whole secret of this chapter.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Okay, let's do an example. Slowly. This one is from your study notebook.

HOST A: In one day, Anna can make at most thirty pizzas, or sixty salads. Ben can make at most twenty pizzas, or twenty salads.

HOST B: Let me repeat that. Anna: thirty pizzas or sixty salads. Ben: twenty pizzas or twenty salads.

HOST A: First question: who has the absolute advantage?

HOST B: Anna makes more pizzas, thirty against twenty. And more salads, sixty against twenty. So Anna has the absolute advantage in both.

HOST A: Exactly. Anna is better at everything.

HOST B: So... why would she ever trade with Ben?

HOST A: [laughs] That's the question. Let's calculate opportunity costs.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: What does one pizza cost Anna, in salads?

HOST B: She can make thirty pizzas or sixty salads. So every pizza costs her two salads.

HOST A: Exactly. Sixty divided by thirty. Two salads per pizza.

HOST B: And for Ben?

HOST A: Twenty pizzas or twenty salads. So one pizza costs Ben one salad.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: So pizza is cheaper for Ben. He gives up only one salad, Anna gives up two.

HOST A: Right. So Ben has the comparative advantage in pizza.

HOST B: And salads?

HOST A: One salad costs Anna half a pizza. One salad costs Ben one pizza. So salad is cheaper for Anna.

HOST B: So Anna has the comparative advantage in salad.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And here's the key rule. In a world with two goods, you can have an absolute advantage in both. But you can never have a comparative advantage in both.

HOST B: Because opportunity costs are mirror images.

HOST A: Exactly. If one pizza costs two salads, then one salad costs half a pizza. They're always reciprocals.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Quick check. Anna's opportunity cost of pizza is two salads. What's her opportunity cost of one salad?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Half a pizza. It's the reciprocal.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: So now they trade. How?

HOST A: Each specialises in the good where they have the comparative advantage. Ben makes pizza, Anna makes salad. Then they exchange.

HOST B: But at what price?

HOST A: Here's the beautiful part. Suppose they agree that one pizza trades for one and a half salads.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: For Ben, making a pizza costs one salad at home. But he can sell it for one and a half salads. He gains.

HOST B: And Anna?

HOST A: At home, a pizza costs Anna two salads. Through trade, she gets one for only one and a half salads. She gains too.

HOST B: [excited] So they're both better off!

HOST A: Both. And any price between their two opportunity costs, so between one and two salads per pizza, makes both better off.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: So that's the answer to our Switzerland puzzle.

HOST A: Exactly. It doesn't matter that Switzerland is better at almost everything. What matters is relative productivity. Even a country that's worse at everything has something it's relatively good at.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Pause and try. Who specialises in what, Anna or Ben?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Ben in pizza, Anna in salad. Each follows their comparative advantage.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: The lecture also shows this on the production-possibility frontier.

HOST A: Yes. In autarky, each person can only consume what they produce. So they stay on their own frontier.

HOST B: And with trade?

HOST A: With specialisation, total production goes up. So together they can consume combinations that were impossible before, outside their own frontiers.

HOST B: And the gains have two names.

HOST A: Right. Material gains from trade: the increase in total production. And subjective gains from trade: the increase in how well-off people feel.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And there's a twist with the shape of the frontier.

HOST A: Yes. So far we used straight lines: constant opportunity costs. But the frontier can bend.

HOST B: Slowly.

HOST A: A concave frontier, bending outwards, means productivity falls the more you produce. If opportunity costs already differ in autarky, it's still better to specialise and trade.

HOST B: And convex?

HOST A: A convex frontier, bending inwards, means productivity rises the more you produce. Like learning by doing. Here's the surprising part: there might be no comparative advantage at the start. But specialisation itself creates one.

HOST B: So you become good at something because you specialise in it.

HOST A: Exactly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now the darker side. Does everyone always win?

HOST A: Not always. The lecture compares two situations.

HOST B: First?

HOST A: Simultaneous integration. Three people, A, B and C, all start in autarky at the same time, and then specialise and trade together. The extra production can be shared so that at least one is better off, and nobody is worse off.

HOST B: No losers.

HOST A: Right. Now the second: sequential integration.

HOST B: Go on.

HOST A: A and B already trade with each other. Then C arrives with a better deal. So A drops B, and trades with C instead.

HOST B: [surprised] And B?

HOST A: B falls back to autarky, but now he's worse off than when he traded. B is a loser from integration.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: That sounds like globalisation.

HOST A: Exactly. And that's why the course says countries can't be treated as single actors. Trade creates winners and losers inside a country, even if the winners could, in principle, compensate the losers.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And there's a risk of dependence.

HOST A: Yes. Giving up autarky makes you vulnerable, because you depend on imports. The example is Switzerland and agriculture. Switzerland has no comparative advantage in farming, but it still produces a lot of its own food, with higher prices and subsidies.

HOST B: To avoid depending on others for food.

HOST A: Exactly. And the course gives two ways to look at it. In a "ceteris paribus" view, you worry about an aggressor making unfair demands. But in a "general equilibrium" view, mutual dependence makes conflicts less likely, because everyone has something to lose.

HOST B: And the hold-up problem appears here too.

HOST A: Right. Specialisation can create power imbalances that the stronger partner can exploit.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And there's even a philosophical point about specialisation.

HOST A: Yes. On the positive side, trade and the pursuit of profit can make society more peaceful. People see each other as trading partners, not enemies.

HOST B: And the negative side?

HOST A: Alienation. The more you specialise, the more you become a small cog in a big machine. The course quotes the worry that very narrow work can make people's minds narrow too. So looking only at material gains is too narrow to understand well-being.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And the chapter ends with institutions.

HOST A: Yes. Institutions are humanly devised rules that structure political, social and economic interactions. And they only exist because people agree on them. The course calls that "ontologically subjective".

HOST B: Like money, or property.

HOST A: Exactly. And the link is: comparative advantage leads to specialisation, and specialised economic activity needs institutions to organise it. That's the topic of next episode.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Let's wrap up.

HOST A: One: absolute advantage means producing more. Comparative advantage means a lower opportunity cost. Two: trade patterns follow comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.

HOST B: Three: you can have an absolute advantage in both goods, but never a comparative advantage in both. Four: any price between the two opportunity costs makes both sides better off. And five: trade can create losers, especially when integration is sequential.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Final quiz. What's the difference between absolute and comparative advantage?

PAUSE: 10

HOST B: Absolute advantage: you produce more of the good. Comparative advantage: your opportunity cost of producing it is lower.

HOST A: Country A can make at most ten units of good one, or twenty units of good two. What's its opportunity cost of one unit of good one?

PAUSE: 10

HOST B: Two units of good two. Twenty divided by ten.

HOST A: True or false: if a country has an absolute advantage in both goods, it can't gain from trade.

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: False. Gains come from comparative advantage, and every country has a comparative advantage in something.

HOST A: And in sequential integration, who can lose?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: The partner who gets dropped, and falls back to autarky.

HOST A: [warmly] Great work. Next time: markets and institutions. Property rights, money, and the nine types of markets.

HOST B: See you there!
