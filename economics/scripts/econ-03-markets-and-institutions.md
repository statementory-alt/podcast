# Episode 3 · Markets and Institutions

HOST B: [curious] Here's a thought experiment. You want to sell your old bike. You find a buyer. But there's no law, no police, no courts, and no money. What happens?

HOST A: Hmm. I'd have to trade the bike for something he has. And hope he doesn't just take it.

HOST B: [laughs] Exactly. Markets look simple, but they rest on a lot of invisible rules. That's today's topic.

HOST A: Welcome back to the Economics A study podcast. Episode three: markets and institutions.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Here's the plan. First, what really happens in a market transaction. Then the institutions every market needs. Then money: why it exists, and why it's worth anything at all. And then the nine types of markets, from many sellers to just one.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Let's start with a definition. What's a market?

HOST B: The course says: a market is an institution that encourages specialisation and exchange. It's the foundation of modern capitalist societies.

HOST A: And every transaction on a market has two sides.

HOST B: Yes. Let's take your bike. The physical side: the bike goes from you to the buyer, and something comes back. Goods, services, or information change hands.

HOST A: And the legal side?

HOST B: Rights change hands. You give up your right to the bike, and the buyer gets it. That's written, or implied, in a contract.

HOST A: So buying something is really buying rights.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: So what institutions does a market need?

HOST B: Let's go one by one. First, private property rights.

HOST A: What are they?

HOST B: Your own sphere of control over things. They decide how an object is used, and they create the difference between "mine" and "yours".

HOST A: And without that?

HOST B: Without property rights, there's nothing to exchange. No markets, and no trade.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Are property rights always absolute?

HOST B: Not always. They can be absolute: you can do anything with your object. Or they can be limited by society, if your use harms others or conflicts with moral values. Think of a house: you own it, but you can't do whatever you like with it.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Second institution?

HOST B: Contract law. It sets the rules for making contracts, and for when ownership can be transferred.

HOST A: And property rights and contract law go together.

HOST B: Yes. They're complementary. A market needs both. Property rights say what's yours. Contract law says how you can give it to someone else.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Third: enforcement.

HOST B: Yes. And here's a surprising point from the course. Enforcement doesn't have to come from the state.

HOST A: Really? Who else would enforce contracts?

HOST B: The historical example is the Lex Mercatoria, the law of merchants, in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Merchants made their own rules, judged their own disputes, and enforced the rules themselves.

HOST A: Without a king or a central court?

HOST B: Exactly. And the course says this was one of the key reasons for Europe's economic success. Europe was politically fragmented, so a central state couldn't enforce contracts everywhere. The merchants solved it themselves.

HOST A: And there's a modern parallel.

HOST B: Yes. Today, global companies trade across many countries, and there's no world government to enforce their contracts. A bit like medieval merchants.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And the fourth institution: self-ownership.

HOST B: Self-ownership means you own yourself. It rules out slavery and serfdom.

HOST A: And why does a market need it?

HOST B: Because without it, there can be no labour contracts. You can only sell your work if you own yourself. And self-ownership also limits contracts: you're not allowed to sell yourself into slavery, even voluntarily.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. Name the institutions a market needs.

PAUSE: 8

HOST B: Private property rights, contract law, a way to enforce them, and self-ownership for labour contracts.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a link between law and economics.

HOST B: Yes. The law defines how far markets can develop. And economics can tell lawyers what the likely effects of a legal rule will be. There's a whole field called "Law and Economics".

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Okay, now money. Let's start without it.

HOST B: In a barter economy, goods are exchanged directly for other goods. No money.

HOST A: Like my bike for his guitar.

HOST B: Exactly. And in barter, every person is a buyer and a seller at the same time.

HOST A: So what's the problem?

HOST B: The double coincidence of wants. A trade only happens if I want exactly what you have, and you want exactly what I have, at the same moment.

HOST A: [laughs] That's going to be rare.

HOST B: Very rare. And money solves it. With money, I can sell my bike to anyone, and buy a guitar from someone else.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And money has three functions.

HOST B: Yes, and they're exam favourites. One: a medium of exchange. You use it to buy things. Two: a unit of account. You measure prices in it. Three: a store of value. You can save it for later.

HOST A: Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And here's the strange part. Money has no value in itself.

HOST B: Right. A banknote is just paper. Its value is an abstract promise that you'll be able to swap it for real goods later.

HOST A: So why does it work?

HOST B: Because it's a social convention. Money has value as long as people are willing to accept it. The moment people stop trusting it, its value can drop to zero.

HOST A: And historically, the course says money probably grew out of debt certificates.

HOST B: Yes. And the history goes in steps. Gold and silver coins, with the Lydians, around five to six hundred years before Christ. Then paper money, in seventh century China, and later in Europe. Then banknotes backed by gold. Then, after Bretton Woods, unbacked money. And now, in the digital age, abstract units like Bitcoin.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. What are the three functions of money?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now the types of markets. This is a nice table.

HOST B: Yes. You ask two questions. How many sellers? One, few, or many. And how many buyers? One, few, or many. That gives nine combinations.

HOST A: Let's go slowly through the important ones.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Many sellers and many buyers: polypoly. That's perfect competition.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: Wheat, or the stock market. Everyone is so small that nobody can change the price. They're all price takers.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: One seller, many buyers.

HOST B: Monopoly. The single seller can influence the price.

HOST A: Few sellers, many buyers.

HOST B: Oligopoly. Examples: Swiss supermarkets, Swiss mobile phone networks, or the global aircraft market.

HOST A: And what's special about oligopoly?

HOST B: Strategic interdependence. Each seller has to think about what the others will do. If Migros cuts prices, Coop has to react.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And the mirror images?

HOST B: One buyer and many sellers: monopsony. Few buyers and many sellers: oligopsony.

HOST A: And one seller with one buyer?

HOST B: Bilateral monopoly. Both sides have power. Examples: a highly specialised scientist and her employer. A government and its defence contractor. Or, the course says, marriage.

HOST A: [laughs] Marriage as a bilateral monopoly. Romantic.

HOST B: [laughs] Economists. The key question there is bargaining: who gets what in the negotiation.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And the course mainly studies three of the nine.

HOST B: Yes. Polypoly, monopoly and oligopoly. And they build on each other. Polypoly is the simplest: everyone takes the price. Monopoly adds one layer: the seller can influence the price. Oligopoly adds another layer: strategic interdependence.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's one that's not in the table.

HOST B: Monopolistic competition. It mixes monopoly and polypoly. Many firms make similar, but not identical, products. Each behaves a bit like a monopolist for its own product.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: Car makers. Lots of companies make SUVs, but each one is a bit different.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Your turn. One supplier, few buyers. What's it called?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Restricted monopoly.

HOST A: And airline travel: which structure?

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: Oligopoly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Last part: what determines which market structure you get?

HOST B: Three things. First, the technology of production: how inputs turn into outputs. If production needs huge factories, there will be fewer sellers.

HOST A: Second?

HOST B: How buyers see the products. If buyers can't tell the products apart, they're homogeneous, like wheat. If they can, like with laptops or red wine, each brand has a little market power.

HOST A: And third?

HOST B: The legal framework. Competition law protects a minimum of competition, and stops monopolies. But patent law does the opposite: it gives the inventor a temporary monopoly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: every transaction has a physical side and a legal side. Two: markets need property rights and contract law, which are complementary, plus enforcement, and self-ownership for labour.

HOST A: Three: enforcement doesn't have to be central, as the Lex Mercatoria shows. Four: money solves the double coincidence of wants, has three functions, and has no intrinsic value. And five: there are nine market structures, and the course focuses on polypoly, monopoly and oligopoly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Final quiz. Why are property rights and contract law called complementary?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Because a market needs both. Property rights say what's yours, contract law says how you can transfer it.

HOST B: What problem of barter does money solve?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: The double coincidence of wants.

HOST B: Many sellers, many buyers, homogeneous good. Which market structure?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Polypoly. Perfect competition.

HOST B: And what are the three determinants of market structure?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Technology of production, how buyers perceive the products, and the legal framework.
