# Episode 5 · Normative Economics

HOST B: [curious] Imagine you and a friend both got a five on an exam. Now the teacher offers to change your friend's grade to a five and a half, and leave yours as it is. Is that better?

HOST A: Well... nobody is worse off. My friend is better off.

HOST B: So by one famous economic standard, it's an improvement. But does it feel fair?

HOST A: [laughs] Not really, if we did the same work.

HOST B: And that tension is what today's episode is about. Welcome back to the Economics A study podcast. Episode five: normative economics.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Here's the plan. First, what "efficient" means in economics: Pareto efficiency. Then consumer and producer surplus, as a way to measure welfare. Then two big theorems about markets. And then the philosophy: three ways to judge whether something is right, and where economics fits in.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Let's start with efficiency. The course gives two types.

HOST A: First, efficiency in production. You can't move resources between producers to make more of one good, without making less of another.

HOST B: That's being on the production-possibility frontier.

HOST A: Exactly. Second, efficiency in consumption. You can't move goods between consumers to make one person better off, without making another worse off.

HOST B: And if both are true?

HOST A: Then the allocation is Pareto efficient.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Let's slow down on that. What's an allocation?

HOST A: A specific way of distributing all the goods and resources among the people in an economy. Who gets what.

HOST B: And Pareto efficient means?

HOST A: [slowly] An allocation is Pareto efficient if it's impossible to make anyone better off without making someone else worse off.

HOST B: So there's no waste. No "free" improvement is left.

HOST A: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And a Pareto improvement?

HOST A: A change where at least one person is better off, and nobody is worse off.

HOST B: And Pareto inefficient?

HOST A: An allocation where a Pareto improvement is still possible. You could make someone better off for free.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now our grades example. From your summary. There are three scenarios. In scenario one, you get a five, and your friend gets a five.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: In scenario two, you get a five, and your friend gets a five and a half. In scenario three, you get a six, and your friend gets a four and a half.

HOST A: And we assume higher grades are better, and nobody cares about the other's grade.

HOST B: Right. So which scenarios are Pareto efficient?

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Scenario one is inefficient, because we could improve one person for free. Scenarios two and three are efficient.

HOST B: And which is a Pareto improvement over scenario one?

HOST A: Scenario two. Your friend is better off, and you're not worse off.

HOST B: And scenario three?

HOST A: It's efficient, but it's not a Pareto improvement over scenario one. Because the friend gets worse, from five to four and a half.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And the twist from our opening?

HOST A: Many people feel scenario two is unfair. Same work, different grades. So Pareto efficiency can clash with our sense of justice.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And there's a money example.

HOST A: Yes. In allocation one, Anna gets fifty francs and Ben gets fifty. In allocation two, Anna gets one hundred and Ben gets forty.

HOST B: There's more money in total in allocation two.

HOST A: Right, but moving from one to two is not a Pareto improvement. Ben loses ten francs.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Quick check. Moving from one allocation to another makes three people better off and one person slightly worse off. Is it a Pareto improvement?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: No. For a Pareto improvement, nobody can be worse off.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And what's the main criticism of Pareto efficiency?

HOST A: It's blind to distribution. An allocation where one person has everything, and everyone else has nothing, can still be Pareto efficient.

HOST B: Because you can't help anyone else without taking from that one person.

HOST A: Exactly. So Pareto efficiency ignores distributive justice. The course calls it a minimum criterion: an inefficient allocation can't be just, from a welfare point of view. But being efficient isn't enough to be just.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now surplus. We met it last episode. Let's connect it to efficiency.

HOST A: Every point on the demand curve shows someone's maximum willingness to pay. Consumer surplus is the difference between that and the market price, added up over all buyers.

HOST B: And producer surplus?

HOST A: Every point on the supply curve shows a seller's minimum acceptable price. Producer surplus is the difference between the market price and that, added up over all sellers.

HOST B: And the sum?

HOST A: The course calls it the aggregate rent. Consumer surplus plus producer surplus. It measures the total gains from trade in a market.

HOST B: And the link to Pareto?

HOST A: The allocation that maximises the aggregate rent is Pareto efficient.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Your summary has a calculation. Let's do it slowly.

HOST A: Inverse demand: price equals one hundred minus half of x. Inverse supply: price equals half of x. And the market price is fifty.

HOST B: How much is sold?

HOST A: From the demand side: fifty equals one hundred minus half x. So half x is fifty, and x is one hundred.

HOST B: Consumer surplus?

HOST A: A triangle. The demand curve starts at one hundred, the price is fifty. So the height is fifty. The base is one hundred units. Half of fifty times one hundred: two thousand five hundred.

HOST B: Producer surplus?

HOST A: Supply starts at zero, and goes up to the price of fifty. Height fifty, base one hundred. Also two thousand five hundred.

HOST B: So the aggregate rent is five thousand.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now the two big theorems. This is core exam material.

HOST B: Slowly.

HOST A: The first theorem of welfare economics: every equilibrium in competitive markets is Pareto efficient. So it maximises the sum of consumer and producer surplus.

HOST B: That's Adam Smith's invisible hand, in formal language.

HOST A: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And what does it not say?

HOST A: Good question, because exams test this. It doesn't say the equilibrium is unique. It doesn't say markets are the only way to reach efficiency. And it doesn't say the market maximises only consumer surplus, or only producer surplus. It maximises the sum.

HOST B: And it needs conditions.

HOST A: Right. Your notebook stresses this. It only holds under perfect competition, without externalities, without public goods, and with full information. When those fail, markets can be inefficient. We'll see that in later chapters.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And the second theorem?

HOST A: Under certain conditions, every Pareto efficient allocation can be reached through markets, if you first redistribute what people start with.

HOST B: In plain words?

HOST A: If you don't like how things are distributed, don't mess with prices. Redistribute people's starting resources, and then let the market do its job.

HOST B: So efficiency and fairness don't have to clash.

HOST A: In theory, no. If you can redistribute like that, there's no tension between efficiency and distributive justice.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And how do markets guide resources, without a planner?

HOST A: Through prices. When people buy and sell, they reveal what they want. And prices signal how scarce things are. So nobody needs to know everything. Each person only needs the prices that matter to them.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: But the second theorem has limits.

HOST A: Three. First, incentives. Redistribution can change behaviour. For example, people may work less if they're taxed more. Second, enforcement. Whoever redistributes needs enough independence and power to do it. And third, decentralisation. It's odd to redistribute centrally, but then insist that everything else be decentralised.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Quick check. What does the first welfare theorem say?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Every equilibrium in a competitive market is Pareto efficient.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now the philosophy. Three families of normative theories.

HOST A: Let's take them one at a time.

HOST B: First: consequentialism.

HOST A: Only the consequences of an action matter. If the outcome is good, the action is good. And institutions are seen as incentive systems that push people towards the best outcome for society.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Second: deontology.

HOST A: Consequences don't matter. What matters is the act itself: duties and rights. Kant is the classic example. For Kant, it's not institutions but reason that should guide people.

HOST B: So lying is wrong, even if it leads to a good outcome.

HOST A: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And third: virtue ethics.

HOST A: People should develop good character. A virtuous person acts well not because a rule forces them, but because it's become second nature. And institutions should help people become virtuous.

HOST B: That goes back to Aristotle.

HOST A: Yes. And the course contrasts Aristotle with Machiavelli. Mainstream economics is closer to the idea of institutions as incentive mechanisms: making self-interested people act in society's interest, rather than making them good.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And where does mainstream economics stand?

HOST A: In welfarism. That's a subclass of consequentialism. Only individual welfare, meaning people's own well-being or utility, counts when you judge institutions.

HOST B: So economics is normative too, in a way.

HOST A: Yes. But the course stresses that economists aren't experts in choosing values. Their job is to analyse how specific ideas of fairness can be put into practice with institutions.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And there's John Rawls.

HOST A: Yes: reflective equilibrium. It's the back-and-forth process of adjusting our ethical principles and our judgements about specific institutions, until they fit together. Ideally through discussion between economists, philosophers and the public.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Your turn. "Torture is wrong, even if it would save lives." Which theory?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Deontology. Rights and duties regardless of consequences.

HOST B: "A policy is good if it increases total happiness."

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Consequentialism. More specifically, welfarism.

HOST B: "Schools should teach children to be honest and brave."

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Virtue ethics.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: And the last idea: revealed preference.

HOST A: The revealed preference approach says: we can read people's preferences from what they choose. And it assumes people's choices are always in their own best interest.

HOST B: And if we accept that?

HOST A: Then there's no reason to interfere with people's choices.

HOST B: And if we reject it?

HOST A: Then we open the door to paternalism: interventions that limit people's freedom "for their own good". And surplus is no longer a good measure of welfare.

HOST B: Where do people often not act in their own interest?

HOST A: The course mentions financial decisions, like retirement savings or buying on credit, nutrition, like obesity, and anything that needs financial literacy, long-term thinking, or self-control.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: Pareto efficient means you can't make anyone better off without making someone worse off. Two: a Pareto improvement makes at least one person better off, and nobody worse off. Three: Pareto ignores fairness of distribution.

HOST A: Four: the first welfare theorem says competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient. The second says any efficient allocation can be reached after redistributing endowments. And five: mainstream economics is welfarist, a type of consequentialism.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Final quiz. What's the main criticism of Pareto efficiency?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: It ignores the distribution of welfare, so it says nothing about fairness.

HOST B: Allocation A gives Lea two and Tom two. Allocation B gives Lea four and Tom four. Is moving from A to B a Pareto improvement?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Yes. Both are better off.

HOST B: Name the three limits of the second welfare theorem.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Incentives, enforcement, and decentralisation.

HOST B: And mainstream economics belongs to which normative tradition?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Welfarism, a type of consequentialism.
