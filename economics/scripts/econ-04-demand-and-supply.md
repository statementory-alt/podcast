# Episode 4 · Demand and Supply

HOST A: [curious] It's a hot summer day, and suddenly everyone wants iced coffee. What happens to the price, and to how much is sold?

HOST B: Both go up, I'd guess.

HOST A: Right! And now imagine coffee beans get cheaper instead. What happens then?

HOST B: Hmm. The price goes down... and more is sold?

HOST A: Exactly. By the end of this episode, you'll be able to answer questions like that in seconds, with four simple rules.

HOST B: Welcome back to the Economics A study podcast. Episode four: demand and supply.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Here's the plan. First, demand: what drives it, and how to add up individual demands. Then the different types of goods. Then the most confused idea in this chapter: moving along a curve versus shifting the curve. Then supply. Then putting them together in the market equilibrium. And finally, consumer and producer surplus.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Let's start with demand. What decides how much of something I buy?

HOST A: The course lists five things. Let's take ice cream.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: One: the price of the good itself. Two: the prices of other goods. Three: your income and wealth, your budget. Four: your preferences, your taste. And five: your expectations about the future.

HOST B: Price, other prices, budget, taste, expectations.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And in the model, demand is a function. The quantity you buy depends on all the prices, and on your budget.

HOST B: And the course separates two kinds of variables.

HOST A: Yes. Exogenous variables come from outside the model: prices, budget, preferences, expectations. And the endogenous variable is what the model explains: your demand.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now from one person to the whole market.

HOST A: Market demand is the sum of all individual demands. And here's the key: you add them horizontally.

HOST B: What does that mean, in plain words?

HOST A: At each price, you ask: how much does each person buy? And you add up the quantities.

HOST B: Can you give an example?

HOST A: From your summary. At a price of two francs, person A buys six units, and person B buys six units. So the market buys twelve.

HOST B: And at a price of four?

HOST A: A buys four, B buys two. So the market buys six.

HOST B: So: same price, add the quantities.

HOST A: Exactly. And a classic mistake: never add up prices. Adding prices makes no sense.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now types of goods. There's a neat table for this.

HOST A: Yes. Three questions, each with two answers. Let's go slowly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question one: what happens to demand when the good's own price goes up?

HOST B: Normally, people buy less.

HOST A: Right. That's an ordinary good. The most common type.

HOST B: And if people buy more when the price goes up?

HOST A: That's a Giffen good. The course says these often have little real value, and are bought to signal status.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question two: what happens when your income goes up?

HOST B: I buy more?

HOST A: For most goods, yes. That's a normal good.

HOST B: And if I buy less when I get richer?

HOST A: That's an inferior good. Cheap or lower quality things, that you replace with better ones when you can afford it.

HOST B: Like instant noodles.

HOST A: [laughs] Exactly. Or the course's example: plain pasta with pesto.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Question three: what happens when the price of another good goes up?

HOST B: Go on.

HOST A: If the other good gets more expensive, and you buy more of yours, they're substitutes. Like butter and margarine.

HOST B: And if I buy less of mine?

HOST A: Then they're complements. They're used together. Like skis and ski boots, or cars and petrol.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Let me summarise the table. Demand goes up when: income rises for a normal good, own price rises for a Giffen good, another price rises for a substitute. Demand goes down when: income rises for an inferior good, own price rises for an ordinary good, another price rises for a complement.

HOST A: Perfect.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: Quick check. When income rises, people buy less of a good. What type is it?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: An inferior good.

HOST B: And when the price of coffee rises, people buy less sugar. Coffee and sugar are...?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Complements.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now the most confused idea in this chapter. Moving along the curve, versus shifting the curve.

HOST B: Slowly, please. This always confuses me.

HOST A: Here's the rule. On the demand graph, the axes are the good's own price and its quantity.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: If the good's own price changes, you move along the curve. The curve stays where it is. You just slide to a different point.

HOST B: And if anything else changes?

HOST A: Income, other prices, taste, the weather. Then the whole curve shifts. To the right, if people want more at every price. To the left, if they want less.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And there's a language trick.

HOST A: Yes. "Demand" means the whole curve. "Quantity demanded" means one point on it. So when the price of a good rises, demand doesn't fall. The quantity demanded falls.

HOST B: That's subtle.

HOST A: But examiners love it.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Let's practise with bicycles. Helmets get more expensive. What happens to the demand for bicycles?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Helmets and bikes are complements. So demand for bikes falls. The curve shifts left.

HOST B: Bicycles themselves get more expensive.

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: That's the good's own price. So it's a movement along the curve, not a shift.

HOST B: Incomes rise, and bikes are a normal good.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Demand shifts right. People buy more at every price.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Okay, now supply. What decides how much a firm sells?

HOST A: The course lists four things. The price of the good. The prices of the inputs, like labour and materials. The technology used. And the firm's expectations about the future.

HOST B: And the law of supply?

HOST A: Normally, a higher price makes production more profitable, so firms supply more. The supply curve slopes upwards.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And the course takes a little detour into capital.

HOST A: Yes. Capital is any resource that helps you reach a goal. And there are four kinds. Slowly.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: Physical capital: machines and tools. Human capital: your skills. Social capital: your network of friends and contacts. And symbolic capital: your reputation, honour and prestige.

HOST B: Physical, human, social, symbolic.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And market supply is just like market demand. You add up the quantities of all firms, horizontally, at each price.

HOST B: And what shifts the supply curve?

HOST A: Input prices: if production gets more expensive, supply shifts left. Expectations. And technological progress: more output per input, so supply shifts right.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Now we put them together. The market equilibrium.

HOST A: The equilibrium is where the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded. That gives the equilibrium price, and the equilibrium quantity.

HOST B: And if the price is too high?

HOST A: Then there's excess supply. Sellers can't sell everything, so the price falls.

HOST B: And too low?

HOST A: Excess demand. People want more than there is, so the price rises. The price keeps adjusting until the two meet.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Let's calculate one. From your notebook. Demand is five hundred minus five P. Supply is ten P minus two hundred and fifty.

HOST A: Step one: set them equal. Five hundred minus five P equals ten P minus two hundred and fifty.

HOST B: Step two: solve for P.

HOST A: Move things around: seven hundred and fifty equals fifteen P. So P is fifty.

HOST B: Step three: plug it back in.

HOST A: Five hundred minus five times fifty. Five hundred minus two hundred and fifty. So the quantity is two hundred and fifty.

HOST B: Equilibrium price fifty, quantity two hundred and fifty.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And now our four rules, from the start of the episode.

HOST B: Go slowly. These are gold.

HOST A: Demand goes up: price up, quantity up. Demand goes down: price down, quantity down.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: Supply goes up: price down, quantity up. Supply goes down: price up, quantity down.

HOST B: So a demand shift moves price and quantity in the same direction. A supply shift moves them in opposite directions.

HOST A: Exactly.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Back to our opening. A heat wave hits. What happens in the iced coffee market?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Demand rises. The curve shifts right. Price and quantity both go up.

HOST B: And coffee beans get cheaper.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Supply rises. The curve shifts right. The price goes down, and the quantity goes up.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Last topic: consumer and producer surplus.

HOST A: Let's start with consumers. Every point on the demand curve shows the most someone is willing to pay.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: If you'd pay up to eight francs for a coffee, but it costs five, you get three francs of extra value. That's your consumer surplus.

HOST B: And for the whole market?

HOST A: Add it up for everyone. On the graph, it's the area between the demand curve and the price.

PAUSE: 1

HOST B: And producer surplus?

HOST A: The mirror image. Each point on the supply curve shows the lowest price a seller would accept. If a seller would accept three francs, but gets five, that's two francs of producer surplus.

HOST B: And on the graph?

HOST A: The area between the price and the supply curve.

HOST B: And together?

HOST A: Consumer plus producer surplus gives total welfare. The total gains from trade in that market.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Can we calculate one, simply?

HOST A: Sure. Inverse demand: price equals one hundred minus x. Inverse supply: price equals twenty plus x.

HOST B: Equilibrium first.

HOST A: One hundred minus x equals twenty plus x. So x is forty, and the price is sixty.

HOST B: Consumer surplus?

HOST A: It's a triangle. Half times the height times the base. The height is from the top of the demand curve, one hundred, down to the price, sixty. That's forty. The base is the quantity, forty. So half of forty times forty: eight hundred.

HOST B: And producer surplus?

HOST A: From the price, sixty, down to where supply starts, twenty. That's forty again. Half of forty times forty: also eight hundred.

HOST B: So total welfare is one thousand six hundred.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: demand depends on price, other prices, budget, taste and expectations. Two: market demand and supply are added horizontally, at each price.

HOST A: Three: own price changes move you along the curve, everything else shifts it. Four: demand shifts move price and quantity together, supply shifts move them in opposite directions. And five: consumer plus producer surplus is total welfare.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Final quiz. Name the four kinds of capital.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Physical, human, social, and symbolic capital.

HOST B: The price of a good rises because of a new tax on its inputs. Does demand shift?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: No. Supply shifts left, and that raises the price. For demand, it's a movement along the curve.

HOST B: Supply rises. What happens to price and quantity?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Price falls, quantity rises.

HOST B: And what's consumer surplus, in one sentence?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: The difference between what buyers would be willing to pay and the market price, added up over all buyers.

HOST B: [warmly] Great work. Next time: normative economics. When is a market outcome "good"? Pareto efficiency and the welfare theorems.

HOST A: See you there!
