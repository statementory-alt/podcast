# Episode 1 · First Principles

HOST B: [curious] Quick question. Tonight you can go to the cinema, or go out for dinner with friends. Not both. You pick the cinema. What did the cinema cost you?

HOST A: The ticket? Fifteen francs?

HOST B: That's what everyone says. But economists would say: the cinema cost you the dinner with your friends.

HOST A: [laughs] Huh. That's a different way of thinking about price.

HOST B: And that's the very first idea in economics. Welcome to the Economics A study podcast. This is episode one: first principles.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Here's the plan for today. First, what economics is actually about: scarcity and opportunity cost. Then the three levels economists look at. Then a bit of philosophy: can an economic theory ever be "true"? Then models, and the famous production-possibility frontier. And finally, the difference between positive and normative statements, which comes up in exams all the time.

HOST B: And as always, one idea at a time. When we ask a question, try to answer out loud before we do.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Let's start with the definition. What is economics?

HOST B: The course defines it as the study of how people and societies choose to use scarce resources, to produce goods, and to distribute them among different people.

HOST A: The key word being "scarce".

HOST B: Exactly. Scarcity is the starting point of everything.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: So what exactly is scarcity?

HOST B: Scarcity means our wants are bigger than the means we have to satisfy them.

HOST A: Wants and means. Let's define both.

HOST B: Wants are what humans want. Some are basic needs, like food and shelter. Others are more subjective, like the latest phone.

HOST A: And means?

HOST B: Means are the resources and goods that help us satisfy those wants. Money, time, materials, skills.

HOST A: And there's one resource that's always scarce.

HOST B: Time. You can earn more money, but nobody gets more than twenty-four hours a day.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And here's a subtle point from the course. Scarcity isn't really a topic economics studies. It's a method.

HOST B: Right. Economics looks at the whole world through the lens of scarcity. Love, politics, climate, anything. The question is always: given that resources are limited, how do people choose?

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Now the most important tool: opportunity cost.

HOST B: The definition: the opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative you gave up.

HOST A: So back to the cinema.

HOST B: Exactly. The opportunity cost of the cinema is the enjoyment of the dinner, if dinner was your next-best option.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a trap here, right?

HOST B: Two traps. First: opportunity cost is not the price tag. It's what you actually give up.

HOST A: And second?

HOST B: You only count the best alternative. Not all of them added up. Because you could only have done one of them anyway.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's try one from your study notebook. Mia has a free Saturday. She can work in a café and earn one hundred and twenty francs. She can help a friend move, which she values at eighty. Or she can study, which she values at one hundred and fifty. She decides to study. What's her opportunity cost?

PAUSE: 10

HOST B: One hundred and twenty francs. The best option she gave up was the café. We don't add the eighty, because she could only do one thing.

HOST A: And if she had chosen the café instead?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Then the best option she gave up is studying. So the opportunity cost is one hundred and fifty.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Next: three levels of analysis.

HOST B: Economists look at the world on three levels. Let's go slowly.

HOST A: Level one.

HOST B: The individual level. How one person behaves under scarcity. Cinema or dinner. That's microeconomics.

HOST A: Level two.

HOST B: The interaction level. How people's choices affect each other. Like two petrol stations on the same street, setting their prices. Or a market.

HOST A: And level three.

HOST B: The aggregate level. The economy as a whole. Growth, inflation, unemployment. That's macroeconomics.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And they're connected.

HOST B: Yes. Modern macroeconomics is "micro-founded". That means big phenomena, like unemployment, are explained by going back to how individuals behave and interact.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Quick check. "How does a household split its budget between food and rent?" Which level?

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: The individual level. Microeconomics.

HOST A: "Why did Swiss inflation rise last year?"

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: The aggregate level. Macroeconomics.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Now a bit of philosophy. The course has a big idea here: methodological individualism.

HOST B: It's a long name for a simple idea. Every social phenomenon must be explained by going back to the behaviour of individuals.

HOST A: So "society decided" is not an explanation. You have to ask which people decided, and why.

HOST B: Exactly.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And then the famous homo oeconomicus.

HOST B: Yes. Homo oeconomicus is a model of how people make decisions. It's a set of assumptions, not a description of real humans.

HOST A: What does it assume?

HOST B: That people are rational. They have a ranking of what they prefer, called an ordering. And they choose the best option according to that ranking. That's called maximisation.

HOST A: And how do we know what people prefer?

HOST B: Through revealed preference. We look at what people actually choose, and assume that shows what they prefer.

HOST A: So: revealed preference, ordering, maximisation.

HOST B: That's the rational choice paradigm.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: But people aren't always rational.

HOST B: True, and the course admits it. People often move away from their ordering because of other values. But here's an important point: in economics, a good theory doesn't have to rely on realistic assumptions. It has to be useful.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Which brings us to theories. Can an economic theory ever be "true"?

HOST B: The course says: no. Not with certainty. And the reason is called the Münchhausen trilemma.

HOST A: [laughs] Named after the baron who pulled himself out of a swamp by his own hair.

HOST B: Exactly. The trilemma says: if you try to prove any statement, you end up in one of three bad options.

HOST A: Slowly, please.

HOST B: Option one: infinite regress. Every proof needs another proof, and that one needs another, forever.

HOST A: Option two?

HOST B: Circularity. The statement and the proof support each other. For example: "the traits that survive are the best adapted", and "this trait is well adapted, because it survived". That's going in a circle.

HOST A: And option three?

HOST B: Dogmatism. At some point you just stop, and say: these basic assumptions need no proof. That set of shared basic assumptions is called a paradigm.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: So since we can't prove theories true, how do scientists decide?

HOST B: Pragmatically. A theory counts as reasonable if experts in the field agree on it, and on the methods used.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Okay. Now models.

HOST B: A model is a simplified picture of a complicated reality. It's a set of assumptions and hypotheses linked by logic and maths. The theory says what causes what. The model makes sure that idea has no logical contradictions.

HOST A: Like a map. A map isn't the city, but it helps you find your way.

HOST B: Great comparison. And a good model is simple. That's Ockham's razor: with the same explanatory power, prefer the explanation with fewer assumptions.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's a debate about assumptions.

HOST B: Yes. One extreme position is called instrumentalism. It says: assumptions don't matter at all. Only whether the predictions work.

HOST A: And the counter-argument?

HOST B: You could build a model whose assumptions are simply its predictions. It would "predict" perfectly, but it would explain nothing about causes. So if we want to understand why things happen, assumptions do matter.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: And then Karl Popper.

HOST B: Yes, critical rationalism. Popper says: theories can never be proven right. But they can be proven wrong, by finding evidence that contradicts them. That's called falsification.

HOST A: So a theory that hasn't been proven wrong yet...

HOST B: Is "provisionally valid". It survives until someone falsifies it. And most economists work this way.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Quick check. A researcher says: "My model predicts stock prices well, so it doesn't matter that its assumptions are absurd." Which position is that?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Instrumentalism.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now a classic tool: the production-possibility frontier. The PPF.

HOST B: Imagine a country that can only produce two goods. Let's say bread and bikes.

HOST A: Okay.

HOST B: The PPF is a curve showing all the combinations of bread and bikes the country can produce, when it uses all its resources.

HOST A: And what does it show us?

HOST B: Three things. Points on the curve are efficient: you're using everything. Points inside the curve are possible, but inefficient: you're wasting resources. And points outside the curve are impossible, at least for now.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And the curve slopes down.

HOST B: Right. To make more bikes, you have to make less bread. That's opportunity cost again, drawn as a picture.

HOST A: And there was something about increasing opportunity costs.

HOST B: Yes. Often, the more of one good you produce, the more of the other you have to give up for each extra unit. That makes the curve bend outwards.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Now the part that's in almost every exam. Positive versus normative.

HOST B: Positive statements describe what is. They explain cause and effect, without value judgements. And they can be tested.

HOST A: Example?

HOST B: "A wealth tax leads to a more equal distribution of wealth." You could test that with data.

HOST A: And normative?

HOST B: Normative statements say what should be. They're based on values, and can't be tested with data. "Wealth should be distributed equally."

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: Any tips for spotting them?

HOST B: Look for words like "should", "ought", "fair", "good", "too much". Those usually signal a normative statement.

HOST A: And a classic mistake?

HOST B: Thinking "positive" means "true". A positive statement can be false. It's just testable. And normative doesn't mean unscientific. Economics can tell you which tool reaches a goal, once society has chosen that goal.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Your turn, listener. "A higher minimum wage reduces employment among young workers." Positive or normative?

PAUSE: 7

HOST B: Positive. It's a testable claim about effects, even if it might turn out to be false.

HOST A: "It's unfair that managers earn a hundred times more than cleaners."

PAUSE: 6

HOST B: Normative. The word "unfair" is a value judgement.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And there's one more philosophical idea: the naturalistic fallacy.

HOST B: Yes. It says you can't define "good" by pointing to natural facts. Science can describe what happens, but it can't prove that something is good.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Finally, schools of thought.

HOST B: Two main ones in this course. First, neoclassical economics. It assumes rational individuals, and sees society as a network of exchanges driven by self-interest and opportunity costs. It dominates microeconomics.

HOST A: And it introduced marginal thinking.

HOST B: Yes, the marginalist revolution. People compare the benefit of one more unit of something with the cost of that one more unit.

HOST A: And the second school?

HOST B: New institutional economics. It says economic exchanges are embedded in culture, norms and institutions. And it puts transaction costs at the centre, to compare markets, firms and governments.

HOST A: So it doesn't replace neoclassical economics.

HOST B: No. It builds on it, and completes it.

PAUSE: 1

HOST A: And the lecture mentioned four big topics for today's world.

HOST B: Inequality, digitalisation, the climate crisis, and trade wars.

PAUSE: 2

HOST A: Let's wrap up.

HOST B: One: economics is about scarcity, and the key tool is opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative you give up. Two: there are three levels: individual, interaction, and aggregate.

HOST A: Three: theories can never be proven true, because of the Münchhausen trilemma, but they can be falsified. Four: models simplify, and simpler is better. And five: positive statements describe what is, normative statements say what should be.

PAUSE: 2

HOST B: Final quiz. What is opportunity cost?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: The value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice.

HOST B: Name the three horns of the Münchhausen trilemma.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Infinite regress, circularity, and dogmatism.

HOST B: A point inside the production-possibility frontier. What does it mean?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: It's possible, but inefficient. Resources are being wasted.

HOST B: And what does Popper say we can do with theories?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: We can never prove them, but we can falsify them.

HOST B: [warmly] Great job. Next time: gains from trade. Why does it pay to specialise, even if you're worse at everything than your partner?
