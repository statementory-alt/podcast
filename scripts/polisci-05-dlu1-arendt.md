# Episode 5 · DLU 1 · Arendt, Lobbying and Your Innovation

HOST A: Welcome to episode five. This one is different, because week five has no lecture. It's the first Digital Learning Unit, DLU 1.

HOST B: Instead of new material, DLU 1 hits pause, reviews Lectures 1 to 4, and applies them to the term challenge. The assignments aren't graded, but the content is exam-relevant. The focus is on being able to explain the concepts yourself, apply them to concrete observations, and justify different interpretations.

HOST A: And the guiding question is the term challenge: "Which political innovation is best suited to strengthen the representation of future generations' interests in climate policy decision-making processes?"

HOST B: It has four elements, about five hours in total. One: an in-depth reading of Hannah Arendt, then a discussion with Brian, the HSG's Socratic AI tutor, about ninety to a hundred and twenty minutes. Two: an SRF report on lobbying in the Swiss parliament, plus questions on Rflect, about sixty minutes. Three: interviews with practitioners, about sixty minutes. Four: your own innovation proposal, sixty to ninety minutes, submitted on Rflect by the seventeenth of October 2026.

HOST A: You can do it alone or with others, and the lecture hall is open during the usual slot if you want help. Let's start with Arendt, because that's the heaviest part, and the most likely to show up in the exam.

HOST B: The text is chapter two of "On Violence," from 1970. Why this text?

HOST A: Because Arendt's concept of power, power to, complements Weber's classical power over. And because the climate movement keeps drawing on Arendt, especially on power and collective action. The idea is that it might inspire your innovation.

HOST B: So what's her starting point?

HOST A: She notices that political theorists from left to right agree on one thing: violence is just the most flagrant manifestation of power. C. Wright Mills says all politics is a struggle for power and the ultimate kind of power is violence. Weber defines the state as the rule of men over men based on the means of legitimate violence. Bertrand de Jouvenel says the essence of power is command: to command and to be obeyed. And she points out that if the essence of power is effective command, then there's no greater power than the one that grows out of the barrel of a gun. You couldn't tell the order of a policeman from the order of a gunman.

HOST B: And she thinks that whole tradition is a mistake.

HOST A: She explains where it comes from. The idea of absolute sovereignty, with Bodin and Hobbes. The ancient definitions of government as the rule of man over man, by one, a few, the best, or the many. She adds a modern one: bureaucracy, "rule by Nobody," which she calls potentially the most tyrannical of all, because nobody can be held responsible. Then the Hebrew-Christian idea of law as command, generalised from God's commandments. And modern ideas about an instinct of domination.

HOST B: And she pushes back on that instinct.

HOST A: She says the instinct of submission, the desire to obey some strong man, is at least as prominent in human psychology as the will to power. And then she points to another tradition: the Athenian isonomy and the Roman civitas. There, power and law did not rest on command and obedience. The eighteenth-century revolutionaries turned to those models to build a republic, where the rule of law rests on the power of the people and ends the rule of man over man.

HOST B: And here's the famous passage on support.

HOST A: She says the revolutionaries still talked about obedience, obedience to laws instead of men, but what they really meant was support for the laws the citizens had consented to. It's the people's support that gives power to a country's institutions, and that support is the continuation of the consent that created the laws in the first place. Then the key line: "All political institutions are manifestations and materializations of power; they petrify and decay as soon as the living power of the people ceases to uphold them."

HOST B: That's the quote Göhler uses in the institutions reading.

HOST A: Exactly, and it's a great link to make in an exam answer. She also quotes Madison: "all governments rest on opinion." Even a king, even a tyrant, needs helpers.

HOST B: And then numbers.

HOST A: Yes. One of the most obvious distinctions: power always needs numbers, while violence, up to a point, can manage without them, because it relies on implements. The extreme form of power is All against One. The extreme form of violence is One against All, and that's never possible without instruments. Tyranny, as Montesquieu found, is the most violent and least powerful form of government.

HOST B: Then she complains about political science.

HOST A: She says it's a sad reflection on political science that our terminology doesn't distinguish between power, strength, force, authority and violence. Using them as synonyms comes from the conviction that the only political question is "Who rules Whom?" Once you stop reducing public affairs to domination, the real diversity of these phenomena appears. And then she gives five definitions. This is the core of the text.

HOST B: Power.

HOST A: Power corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert. It's never the property of an individual. It belongs to a group and exists only as long as the group stays together. When we say someone is "in power," we mean they're empowered by a certain number of people to act in their name. When the group disappears, "his power" vanishes. Potestas in populo: without a people or a group, there's no power.

HOST B: Strength.

HOST A: Strength is singular. It belongs to an individual person or object, as part of its character. It can be overpowered by the many, who will often combine precisely to ruin someone's independent strength. So when we call someone a "powerful personality," we really mean strong.

HOST B: Force.

HOST A: In everyday speech we use force to mean violence. Arendt wants to reserve it for the "forces of nature" or the "force of circumstances," meaning the energy released by physical or social movements.

HOST B: Authority.

HOST A: Authority can sit in a person, like a parent or teacher, or in an office, like the Roman senate or a priest. Her example: a priest can grant valid absolution even if he's drunk. Its hallmark is unquestioning recognition by those asked to obey. It needs neither coercion nor persuasion. A father can lose his authority by beating his child, behaving like a tyrant, or by starting to argue with him, treating him as an equal. To keep authority you need respect, so the greatest enemy of authority is contempt, and the surest way to undermine it is laughter.

HOST B: And violence.

HOST A: Violence is defined by its instrumental character. It's close to strength, because the tools of violence are designed to multiply natural strength until they can replace it.

HOST B: But she says these never appear in pure form.

HOST A: Right. In reality they combine. Institutionalised power often appears as authority, and nothing is more common than the combination of violence and power. But that doesn't mean they're the same. She explains why we're tempted to equate them: when we think about government, violence looks like the last resort against criminals or foreign enemies, so power looks like the velvet glove over an iron fist.

HOST B: And her evidence against that is revolutions.

HOST A: In a contest of violence against violence, the government always wins. But that superiority lasts only as long as the power structure is intact, as long as commands are obeyed and the army and police are willing to use their weapons. When that stops, the situation changes abruptly, and weapons can change hands within hours, as in the Hungarian revolution. Where commands are no longer obeyed, the means of violence are useless. And whether people obey isn't decided by command and obedience, but by opinion, and by how many people share it. Civil obedience is the outward sign of support and consent.

HOST B: She uses Paris in 1968.

HOST A: A fairly harmless student rebellion revealed how vulnerable the whole French system was. There was no revolution, because nobody, least of all the students, was prepared to seize power. Except de Gaulle, who went to see the army generals in Germany. And what he got was support, not obedience, through concessions, not commands. If commands had been enough, he wouldn't have needed to leave Paris.

HOST B: And the conclusion from that?

HOST A: No government has ever been based only on violence. Even the totalitarian ruler needs a power basis, the secret police and informers. Even slave-owners didn't rule through superior coercion, but through the organised solidarity of the masters. Then she says power is the essence of all government, but violence is not. Violence is instrumental. Like all means, it needs guidance and justification through its end, and what needs justification by something else can't be the essence of anything. Power, by contrast, is "an end in itself." It's the condition that allows a group to think in means and ends at all.

HOST B: And then the legitimacy versus justification passage.

HOST A: This is the one to learn. "Power needs no justification, being inherent in the very existence of political communities; what it does need is legitimacy." Power springs up whenever people get together and act in concert, but it gets its legitimacy from the initial getting together, not from any action that follows. When challenged, legitimacy appeals to the past. Justification relates to an end in the future. So violence can be justifiable, but it will never be legitimate. And the further its intended end lies in the future, the less plausible the justification. Nobody questions violence in self-defence, because the danger is clear and present.

HOST B: What happens when violence and power meet in pure form?

HOST A: Her example is Soviet tanks against the entirely non-violent resistance of the Czechoslovak people in 1968. Violence doesn't depend on numbers or opinions but on implements. So in a head-on clash, violence wins. If Gandhi's non-violent resistance had faced Stalin's Russia or Hitler's Germany instead of England, the result wouldn't have been decolonisation but massacre and submission. That's where the famous line comes: "Violence can always destroy power; out of the barrel of a gun grows the most effective command, resulting in the most instant and perfect obedience. What never can grow out of it is power."

HOST B: But violence has a cost.

HOST A: Substituting violence for power can bring victory, but the price is paid by the victor too, in his own power. Loss of power tempts rulers to use violence, and violence itself leads to impotence. The extreme is terror, which appears when violence has destroyed all power but stays in control. Terror depends on atomising society, so no organised opposition is left. And it eventually turns against its own supporters.

HOST B: And her final summary?

HOST A: Power and violence aren't just different. They're opposites. Where one rules absolutely, the other is absent. Violence appears where power is in jeopardy, but left to itself it ends in power's disappearance. So the opposite of violence isn't non-violence. "Non-violent power" is actually redundant. Violence can destroy power, but it's utterly incapable of creating it.

HOST B: Let's do the comparison the DLU asks for. How does Arendt differ from Weber and Popitz?

HOST A: Weber defines power as imposing your will against resistance: power over, a probability, held by someone over someone else. Popitz grounds power in four anthropological capacities and dependencies, including the capacity to harm, which is power of action. For Arendt, harming isn't power at all, it's violence. Power isn't held by an individual, it belongs to a group acting together. It's symmetrical, not asymmetrical. It's freedom-enabling, not freedom-restricting. And it needs legitimacy, not justification, which flips Popitz's third premise that power needs justification because it limits freedom.

HOST B: And for the challenge?

HOST A: Future generations are the perfect hard case for Arendt. Power springs from people getting together and acting in concert. But future generations can't get together. They don't exist yet. So the question is who can act in concert on their behalf, and whether that can ever be legitimate in her sense. The climate strikes are a good example of Arendtian power: no weapons, no sanctions, just numbers acting together.

HOST B: Element two: the SRF Reporter episode "Lobbying im Bundeshaus," about thirty minutes, with English subtitles on YouTube.

HOST A: It follows lobbying on climate policy in the Swiss Federal Parliament, using the national electricity law as the example. The DLU tells you to watch for which actors appear, what means they use to exert influence, and how that relates to the concepts of power. The underlying question is whether you could create a "lobby" for future generations, and whether that would close the representation gap.

HOST B: There are four Rflect questions.

HOST A: One: in ten minutes, note three key points from the report. Not details, but overarching patterns, linked to course content. Two: recall the definition of an institution from Lecture 4, and pick two institutions in the report, ideally one you wouldn't have recognised as an institution at first. Explain with Göhler's definition why they're institutions and what function they have in the process. Three: take one situation from the report and examine it with Weber, Popitz and Arendt. What does each concept make visible, and where do questions stay open? Four: without notes, explain in three to four sentences how one course concept helps you understand the report, then check against your notes and correct.

HOST B: Some tips for question two. Remember: rules, application, awareness. Informal ones count. An informal practice in the corridors of parliament can be an institution as long as the rules are applied and people know them. That's Göhler, and also Lukes' second dimension, the corridors of power.

HOST A: And for question three, instrumental power is the obvious fit for lobbying: promises of support, warnings of job losses, campaign money. Authoritative power fits expertise and standard-setting. Arendt's power to fits coalitions acting together. Weber helps you ask who prevailed against resistance.

HOST B: Element three: the practitioner interviews. Four videos with five people. Fabienne Thomas, whom you already know from the SRF report, interviewed at her workplace in Bern. Her links go to AEE Suisse and the idea of "Enkeltauglichkeit," roughly "fit for the grandchildren." The question to her was whether you can lobby for future generations.

HOST A: Then Elena Müller and Nora Zürcher, both HSG graduates and the current Climate Youth Delegates of Switzerland, asked what that role involves and whether they see themselves as lobbyists for future generations. The linked organisations include the Swiss youth delegates programme through SAJV, Swiss Youth for Climate, the Youth Negotiators Academy, the EU Council on Intergenerational Fairness, focusTerra at ETH Zurich, oikos St. Gallen and Student Impact.

HOST B: Then Katrin Cometta.

HOST A: An HSG alumna from the predecessor programme of the BIA, who headed the Department of the Environment for the city of Winterthur from 2020 to 2026. She talks about how climate policy shows up at local level, the potential for innovation in local government, how innovations get there, and the role of the executive. Links include the 31 Days challenge, Winterthur's climate week, the city's climate protection office and the Zurich heating law.

HOST B: And Nicola Forster.

HOST A: A Swiss founder and political innovator, known for showing that start-ups can serve the common good, not just profit. He talks about what makes a good political innovation. The task: take notes, pick one example from the interviews, research it for no more than ten minutes, and note a source and an open question about its conditions or limits.

HOST B: Element four: your proposal. Max six hundred words, fully developed text, not bullet points. You can use AI, following HSG's AI guidelines, but then you have to explain in a separate Rflect question how you used it and what you gained. If you work in a group, list everyone's names at the end.

HOST A: And it must address four points. One, a brief description of the innovation. Two, a power analysis: whose power is strengthened or weakened, and how? Three, institutional anchoring: how does the innovation fit into existing institutions, or challenge them? Four, ideological assumptions: which ideologies could your idea draw on to build a majority?

HOST B: So it's literally Lectures 2, 4 and 3 in that order.

HOST A: Exactly. Let's sketch how you'd use each lecture. For power, use the vocabulary precisely. Does your idea give someone instrumental power, like a veto or a budget? Authoritative power, like the right to set standards or publish assessments? Power of data constitution, like locking in infrastructure? Or does it create Arendt's power to, by bringing people together to act in concert? And who loses power? Also think about Lukes: does it change who decides, what's on the agenda, or how preferences form?

HOST B: For institutions.

HOST A: Check Göhler's three conditions: would there be rules, would they be applied, would people know about them? Is it formal or informal? Is it an institution-personne, like a new body, or an institution-chose, like a constitutional clause? Does it strengthen steering, integration, or both? Think about veto players and path dependence: what existing institutions could block it, and is there a critical juncture you could use? And remember lesson one from Lecture 4, locking in climate pathways, and lesson two, de-institutionalising polluting ones.

HOST B: And ideology.

HOST A: Use Lecture 3's climate table. Liberals might support it if it's framed as extending liberty over time, like the German Constitutional Court ruling in 2021. Conservatives might support it as stewardship across generations, Burke's partnership of the living, the dead and those yet to be born. Socialists might support it if it's framed as a just transition. Ecologists would ask whether it moves nature towards the core. The point is coalition-building: organising a majority means accommodating people with different views.

HOST B: How is it judged?

HOST A: Strict logical reasoning and persuasiveness, originality and creativity, clarity of presentation, and, less centrally at this stage, feasibility. You don't need a brand new invention, and you don't need to prove it beats every alternative. The lecturers pick twelve to fifteen strong proposals and send them to a practice partner, who gives feedback. You'll hear that feedback in DLU 2, which is about political strategy: what it takes to actually get an innovation through the political process. So you'll first learn about the policy process in the week 7 lecture.

HOST B: Final quiz on Arendt. Name her five terms.

HOST A: Power, strength, force, authority, violence.

HOST B: What does power need, justification or legitimacy?

HOST A: Legitimacy, which comes from people getting together. Violence can be justified, but never legitimate.

HOST B: What's the extreme form of power, and of violence?

HOST A: Power: All against One. Violence: One against All, which needs instruments.

HOST B: What's the greatest enemy of authority?

HOST A: Contempt. And the surest way to undermine it is laughter.

HOST B: Complete the sentence. Violence can always destroy power, but...

HOST A: It's utterly incapable of creating it.
