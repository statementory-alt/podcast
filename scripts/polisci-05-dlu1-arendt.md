# Episode 5 · DLU 1 · Arendt, Lobbying and Your Idea

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome to episode five! This one's different. Week five has no lecture. Instead, there's an online unit, DLU 1.

HOST B: [curious] And what's the point of it?

HOST A: No new topics. It's a chance to review lectures one to four and use them on the big challenge: what new idea would best protect future generations in climate politics?

HOST A: It's not graded, but it can come up in the exam. And you have to hand in your own idea, by the seventeenth of October.

HOST B: Okay. What's in it?

HOST A: Four parts. One: reading Hannah Arendt and discussing it with Brian, the university's AI tutor. Two: a TV report about lobbying in the Swiss parliament. Three: video interviews with people working in climate politics. And four: writing your own proposal.

HOST B: Let's start with Arendt. Why her?

HOST A: Because her idea of power is the opposite of Weber's. And climate activists love quoting her.

HOST A: She starts by noticing that almost everyone, from left to right, thinks violence is just the strongest kind of power. Max Weber even said the state is based on violence.

HOST B: And she disagrees.

HOST A: Strongly. She says: if power were just giving orders and being obeyed, then a robber with a gun would be the most powerful person around. A robber's order and a police officer's order would be the same thing.

HOST A: So she says there's another tradition, from ancient Athens and Rome, and the American revolution. There, power means people supporting their laws, not just obeying orders.

HOST B: Support, not obedience.

HOST A: Yes. Her key line: all political institutions are expressions of power, and they decay as soon as the living power of the people stops holding them up.

HOST B: [excited] Wait, Göhler quoted that in the institutions lecture!

HOST A: He did! Great link to use in an exam answer. And she adds: power always needs numbers. Violence doesn't, because it uses tools, like weapons.

HOST B: So the extreme of power is everyone against one person...

HOST A: And the extreme of violence is one person, with a weapon, against everyone.

HOST B: She also has five words she wants us to stop mixing up, right?

HOST A: Yes. This is the heart of the text. Power, strength, force, authority, and violence.

HOST B: Okay, one by one. Power?

HOST A: The ability of people to act together. It belongs to a group, never to one person. If the group falls apart, the power disappears.

HOST B: Strength?

HOST A: Something one person or thing has on its own. Like a strong personality. When we say "a powerful person", Arendt says we really mean strong.

HOST B: Force?

HOST A: She wants to keep that word for nature. The force of a storm, or the force of circumstances.

HOST B: Authority?

HOST A: [laughs] My favourite. Authority means people obey you without being forced and without being persuaded. Like a respected teacher. She says a father loses authority if he hits his child, or if he starts arguing with him as an equal.

HOST B: And how do you destroy authority?

HOST A: Contempt. And the surest way... is laughing at it.

HOST B: [laughs] Noted. And violence?

HOST A: Violence is a tool. It uses instruments to multiply strength.

HOST B: Quick check for the listener. Name Arendt's five terms.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Power, strength, force, authority, and violence.

HOST B: How does she prove violence isn't the same as power?

HOST A: With revolutions. A government's army always beats a crowd... as long as the soldiers obey. The moment soldiers stop following orders, the weapons are useless. Sometimes they switch sides within hours.

HOST B: So it all depends on whether people support the government.

HOST A: Exactly. She says no government has ever been based only on violence. Even dictators need loyal helpers.

HOST B: And then there's the legitimacy thing.

HOST A: Yes, learn this one. [slowly] Power doesn't need to be justified, but it needs legitimacy. Legitimacy comes from people coming together in the first place.

HOST B: And violence?

HOST A: Violence can sometimes be justified, like self-defence. But it can never be legitimate.

HOST A: And her most famous line: violence can always destroy power. A gun gets you instant obedience. But power can never grow out of a gun.

HOST B: So power and violence are... opposites?

HOST A: Exactly. Where one rules completely, the other is missing. Violence shows up when power is slipping away. And if it takes over, power disappears completely.

HOST B: Pause and answer. According to Arendt, what does power need: justification, or legitimacy?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Legitimacy. Violence can be justified, but never legitimate.

HOST B: How is she different from Weber and Popitz?

HOST A: For Weber and Popitz, power is "power over". One side controls the other, and power limits freedom. Popitz even says the ability to hurt people is a basic kind of power. For Arendt, hurting people isn't power at all. Power is people acting together as equals, and it creates freedom.

HOST B: And for the challenge?

HOST A: Here's the tricky bit. Power comes from people coming together. But future generations can't come together. They don't exist yet.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] So who acts together on their behalf?

HOST A: That's the question for your proposal.

HOST B: Part two: the lobbying report.

HOST A: It's a Swiss TV report, about thirty minutes, with English subtitles. It follows lobbyists in the Swiss parliament, working on the electricity law. Watch who's involved, what tools they use, and how that connects to the kinds of power we learned.

HOST B: And there are four questions on Rflect.

HOST A: Yes. One: note three big patterns from the report. Two: find two institutions in it, using Göhler's three conditions. Three: look at one scene through Weber, Popitz and Arendt. Four: explain one course idea from memory, then check your notes.

HOST A: Tips: for institutions, remember: rules, used, known. Unwritten habits in parliament count too. For power, lobbyists promising support or warning about job losses is instrumental power. Experts setting standards is authoritative power. And groups working together is Arendt's power.

HOST B: Part three: the interviews.

HOST A: Five people. Fabienne Thomas, the lobbyist from the report, asked whether you can lobby for future generations. Elena Müller and Nora Zürcher, both HSG graduates and Switzerland's climate youth delegates. Katrin Cometta, who ran Winterthur's environment department. And Nicola Forster, a founder and political innovator, on what makes a good political idea.

HOST B: And part four: your proposal.

HOST A: Maximum six hundred words. Full sentences, no bullet points. You can use AI, but you have to explain how you used it.

HOST B: And it has to cover four things.

HOST A: Yes. One: describe your idea. Two: power. Whose power gets stronger or weaker? Three: institutions. How does it fit into existing institutions, or challenge them? Four: ideology. Which ideologies could support it, to win a majority?

HOST B: [excited] So that's literally lectures two, four and three!

HOST A: Exactly. For power, name the kind: threats and rewards, setting standards, building things, or people acting together. For institutions, check rules, used, known, and think about lock-in. For ideology, frame it for everyone. For liberals, it protects future freedom. For conservatives, it's looking after things for our grandchildren. For socialists, it's a fair transition.

HOST B: How is it judged?

HOST A: Clear logic, originality, clarity, and a little bit of how realistic it is. The best twelve to fifteen ideas get feedback from a real practitioner in the next online unit.

HOST B: Final quiz! According to Arendt, does power belong to individuals or to groups?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: To groups. Only while they stick together.

HOST B: What's the greatest enemy of authority?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Contempt. And the best way to destroy it is laughter.

HOST B: What are the four things your proposal has to cover?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Your idea, a power analysis, how it fits into institutions, and which ideologies could support it.

HOST B: Last one. Complete the sentence. Violence can always destroy power, but...

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: It can never create it.

HOST B: [warmly] And that's the series so far! Politics, power, ideology, institutions, and Arendt.

HOST A: Good luck with your proposal, and with the exam. Thanks for listening!
