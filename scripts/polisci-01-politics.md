# Episode 1 · What Is Politics?

HOST A: Welcome to the Political Science A study podcast. This is episode one, and we're covering Lecture 1: What is politics? Plus the reading by Werner Patzelt and everything you need to know about how the course works.

HOST B: And we're going to keep this exam-focused. The exam is closed book, digital, a hundred and twenty minutes, on the fourth of February 2027. So everything today is something you could be asked to define, apply, or argue about.

HOST A: Let's start with the course itself, because the lecturers made a point of it. The aim of Political Science A is to give you tools to navigate an increasingly complex political landscape. They name four trends that shape that landscape: polarization, global power shifts, technological change, and the ecological crisis.

HOST B: The structure is twelve weeks, twelve modules. Nine lectures, plus three digital learning units, called DLUs. Every week there's a reading to prepare, and a follow-up through reflection questions and learning self-assessment questions. Three ECTS credits means about ninety hours, roughly five hours a week, plus around thirty hours of exam preparation.

HOST A: And this is important: the exam covers three things. One, the preparatory readings. Two, the lecture contents. Three, the contents of the DLUs. The reflection tasks and self-assessment questions are explicitly described as exam preparation, and selected self-assessment questions will come back in a trial exam on Canvas at the end of the semester.

HOST B: There's also one thing running through the whole term, which is the challenge question. Can you read it out?

HOST A: "Which political innovation is best suited to strengthen the representation of the interests of future generations in climate politics?" Climate politics is the running case study in every lecture. And Lecture 1 opens with a picture from Kyoto, Japan, on the eleventh of December 1997, when around a hundred and sixty nations adopted the Kyoto Protocol. That's the example of politics in action that sets the scene.

HOST B: Okay. So, the big question. What is politics?

HOST A: The lecture starts with why we need politics at all. Following Heywood and Laing, politics arises from three conditions. First, choice, or freedom, as opposed to necessity or natural laws. If everything were determined, there would be nothing to decide. Second, diversity, or disagreement. People hold different views and different interests. Third, scarcity. Resources are limited, so someone has to decide how they're allocated.

HOST B: Choice, disagreement, scarcity. And remember those three, because Lecture 3 comes back to them. Once you know people disagree, the next question is what they disagree about, and that's where ideologies come in.

HOST A: Next, the most useful distinction of the whole lecture, and probably of the whole course: polity, politics and policy. Polity is the structural or institutional framework, the "political system" within which political action takes place. Politics is the political process: contestation, negotiation and decision-making. Policy is the substantive content of governmental action: goals, regulations, instruments.

HOST B: A quick way to remember it. Polity is the form, the rules of the game. Politics is the process, the game being played. Policy is the content, what comes out of it.

HOST A: And Patzelt adds some nice depth here in the reading. He says political processes can be split into sub-processes: will-formation, where it's clarified what should become binding; decision-making, where decisions are actually taken; and implementation, where decisions are translated into concrete measures. Will-formation and decision-making very often take the form of conflict. Implementation does too, whenever it provokes resistance from the people affected.

HOST B: And he has a lovely image for polity.

HOST A: He calls political structures the "riverbed" of political processes. They channel what flows through them. But, and this is his key point, structures are only temporarily stable products of processes. They exist only as long as the processes that produce and sustain them keep running. So political science shouldn't treat institutions as facts of nature.

HOST B: Now the definitions. There are two you need to know word for word.

HOST A: The working definition from Heywood and Laing: politics is "the activity through which people make, preserve and amend the general rules under which they live."

HOST B: And Patzelt's definition, from page eleven: politics is "human action aimed at establishing and enforcing generally binding rules and decisions," in other words "generally binding commitments," "within and between groups of people."

HOST A: The lecture broke Patzelt's definition into five elements, and this is exactly how exam questions are built. One, it's human action. Two, it's "aimed at," which means it doesn't have to succeed. Three, it's about establishing and also enforcing. Four, the rules or decisions are generally binding. And five, it happens within and between groups, not only states. A shared flat counts.

HOST B: Let's do the self-assessment question on this right now, because it's a classic. Which of these can plausibly fall under Patzelt's concept of politics? A, the division of labour in a bee colony. B, wearing a "There is no Planet B" T-shirt in public. C, wearing the same T-shirt at home, in a one-person household. D, a flat-share discussion about the cleaning rota that comes to nothing, for now.

HOST A: The answer is: B and D, yes. A and C, no. The bees fail because it isn't human action. The T-shirt at home fails because a slogan nobody sees isn't aimed at establishing or enforcing generally binding rules. The public T-shirt counts, because it's advocating in public for prioritising the natural foundations of life. And the flat-share meeting counts, because it's aimed at a generally binding decision, even if none is reached. The fact that it's "only" a flat is irrelevant.

HOST B: So "aimed at" and "within groups of people" are doing a lot of work in that question.

HOST A: Exactly. Now, Patzelt also gives you a scheme for analysing any political situation. It's called PINC in English, or MINK in German: Power, Ideology, Norms, Communication. In political action these four always interact.

HOST B: Go through them quickly.

HOST A: Power, following Max Weber, is the chance to impose your will in a social relationship, even against resistance. Patzelt adds that power is exercised not only to impose, but also to prevent, and to shape the very concepts and ways of thinking we use to discuss what should be imposed or prevented. Keep that in mind for Lecture 2.

HOST B: Ideology is the one that trips people up.

HOST A: Yes. For Patzelt, ideology is a belief about the nature and functioning of the reality to be shaped. It's the actors' "perceptual reality." It may not match the "operative reality," but it's unavoidably taken as the basis for action, often aggressively and dismissively. There's a self-assessment question that asks exactly this: according to Patzelt, ideology describes the operative reality, the power reality, the perceptual reality, or the politics reality of the actors. The answer is the perceptual reality.

HOST B: And he links that to the Thomas Theorem: if people define a situation as real, its consequences are real, even if the definition was completely illusory.

HOST A: Norms are formal and informal rules that guide one's own action and create roles and structures like organisations and institutions. They're also the patterns we use to interpret and respond to what others do. And communication, quoting Norbert Wiener, is "the glue that holds society together," whether it's personal, in the press, on TV, websites or social media.

HOST B: Patzelt also mentions a second scheme, AGIL, from Talcott Parsons. It's less central in the lecture, but it's in the reading, so it's fair game.

HOST A: AGIL names four tasks every living system, including a party, a parliament or a state, has to fulfil. A is adaptation to changing environments. G is goal attainment. I is integration, keeping your parts and actions coherent. And L is latent pattern maintenance, keeping the cultural patterns and values that guide your action alive.

HOST B: Okay. Then the lecture zooms out to a map of how different thinkers understand politics. This is from Kreide and Niederberger.

HOST A: Two axes. The first asks whether politics is an activity, meaning human action, or a sphere, meaning an institutional order. The second asks whether politics is essentially, even existentially, conflictual, or oriented towards consensus and compromise.

HOST B: And the thinkers placed on it?

HOST A: On the activity and conflict side: Machiavelli, Carl Schmitt, Chantal Mouffe, who comes back in week six, and Max Weber, week two. On the activity and consensus side: Hannah Arendt, weeks two and five, and Jürgen Habermas, week six. On the institutional order side, oriented to consensus: Aristotle and Niklas Luhmann. The key takeaway isn't memorising every dot. It's that thinkers disagree on two things: whether politics is an activity or a sphere, and whether it's fundamentally about conflict or about consensus.

HOST B: Then we get to climate politics.

HOST A: The lecture applies Heywood's definition directly. Climate politics is "the making, preserving and amending of the general rules" that either seek to limit anthropogenic climate change, which is mitigation, and or address the adverse consequences of climate change, which is adaptation.

HOST B: And it shows up in every quadrant of that map. COP negotiations, street protest, a city's climate budget, the International Court of Justice.

HOST A: Then there are the four grand questions of climate politics. One: what rules are suited to address the systemic risks implied by climate change? The slide showed insurers dropping homeowners in high-risk areas. Two: who pays for the costs of climate change? Three: which technologies, industries and regions should benefit from climate policy? And four: who decides how rules are made in the first place?

HOST B: That fourth one is basically a polity question, right? It's about the rules for making rules.

HOST A: Good link. And it connects straight to the term challenge, because future generations have no say in how those rules are made.

HOST B: Let's move to the second half of the reading: what makes political science a science?

HOST A: Patzelt's definition, page eighteen: "Science is human action that aims to produce statements that are superior in terms of empirical and logical truth content to those statements that can already be formulated using the faculties of common sense."

HOST B: So the bar is: better than common sense. Not certainty, not proof.

HOST A: Exactly, and he says that on purpose. More ambitious definitions demand provability or truth, and plenty of good science never met that standard. So don't demand more than science can deliver, but insist on the minimum.

HOST B: He also explains why everyday thinking isn't good enough. Three features.

HOST A: First, everyday thinking is unreflectively selective. Nobody perceives everything, nobody perceives everything they could, and we forget most of what we did perceive. And we don't think about our selection criteria. This produces two classic fallacies. The individualistic fallacy is generalising from a few known cases to the whole. The ecological fallacy is the mirror image: assuming every member of a group has the group's average characteristics. Political science counters selectivity with random, quota-based or theory-guided case selection.

HOST B: Second?

HOST A: Everyday thinking is unreflectively perspectival. A city looks different from a plane, from the river, from the cathedral tower, but the city doesn't change. Different perspectives produce different "world-views," which show up as different political opinions. Political science counters this with research-guiding concepts and theories.

HOST B: And third, everyday thinking feels natural and unproblematic.

HOST A: Right. When something doesn't fit, people doubt the information rather than their own picture of reality. Political science counters this with epistemology and methodology. Patzelt calls the result "critical knowledge of order and ordering."

HOST B: The lecture also contrasted two classic perspectives on political science.

HOST A: Hobbes, 1651: the skill of making and maintaining commonwealths "consisteth in certain Rules, as doth Arithmetique and Geometry," not on practice only, like tennis. So politics follows learnable rules. And Tocqueville: the destinies of the world proceed as the result, "but often as the contrary result," of the intentions behind them, like a kite that flies through the opposing pull of the wind and the cord. So outcomes are often unintended results of opposing forces.

HOST B: Rules you can learn, versus unintended consequences. Nice pair.

HOST A: Now, Patzelt's four challenges for political science research. These are very likely exam material. One, the complexity of political reality. Two, the historical nature of political reality. Three, the competing self-knowledge of the research subjects. Four, the political scientists' own connections with their object of study.

HOST B: Unpack them a bit.

HOST A: Complexity: lots of people with free will interacting, with meanings shaped by culture, history, region, biography and class. You can't just cut the object down to something simple, because then you get one-sided, distorted conclusions. He even says this frustrates students and makes some drop out in spirit.

HOST B: Historical nature.

HOST A: Political reality is always in flux, so any description is an interim result. That raises the question of whether you can generalise at all. The idiographic approach says you can only describe individual cases. The nomothetic approach looks for general statements. Patzelt says you settle this case by case, by stating clearly which time and place your statements refer to and comparing across points in time. And he firmly rejects "laws of history," like the ones historical materialism claimed to have found.

HOST B: Why?

HOST A: Two reasons. Trends are always discovered by researchers through their own selection and interpretation. And more fundamentally, future change depends on people's future knowledge and interpretations, which can't be foreseen, and people can react to predictions in ways that make them false. So for the past you describe trends and patterns, and for the future you make fallible forecasts.

HOST B: Third, competing self-knowledge.

HOST A: The people you study already have their own everyday knowledge about politics, and that knowledge is part of political reality. So when political science agrees with it, it sounds trivial: why research what everyone knows? And when it disagrees, it sounds wrong or strange. That fuels doubts about whether political science is scientific at all.

HOST B: And fourth, the researchers' own connections.

HOST A: Political scientists share the self-knowledge of the people they study, which helps but can also blunt curiosity. Their agenda gets set by current problems, which come and go. Their research has to be tolerated and funded by the political system, so the object of study shapes the discipline. And their findings feed back into reality: on a small scale through surveys and interviews, on a large scale through things like election forecasts, which can become self-fulfilling or self-refuting.

HOST B: There's a self-assessment question that uses the Afterword of the Patzelt reading. Sandra, a politically engaged schoolgirl loyal to her party, starts studying political science. Discuss one challenge and one broadening of her horizon.

HOST A: The challenge: she'll find it harder to just "politicise" like before. Political science makes you question cherished arguments, distrust knowledge you took for granted, and notice ambiguity in the news. She might develop reservations about her own party's position. Patzelt describes this as a kind of moulting, a "creative crisis."

HOST B: And the broadened horizon?

HOST A: She'll start seeing value in other parties' positions, think more abstractly, and place observations into analytical frameworks. She'll be able to situate her own and others' positions ideologically, see familiar conflicts in a new light, and maybe spot new possible alliances. Patzelt's signs that you've arrived include automatically looking for the power relations, ideologies, norms and communication flows in any issue, and starting answers with "there are at least three aspects to consider."

HOST B: Which, honestly, is a great exam technique too.

HOST A: Let's wrap with the lecture's four conclusions. One: political science is not a method, unlike economics. It's a multi-perspectival discipline aimed at understanding and explaining how individuals and organisations interact to produce, preserve, implement, challenge and amend collective rules and decisions. Two: politics is a lot more than what politicians do. Three: when analysing political phenomena, distinguish polity, politics and policy. Four: climate politics is closely intertwined with other political challenges.

HOST B: Quick-fire quiz. I'll ask, you pause and answer, then we'll say it. What are the three conditions that create the need for politics?

HOST A: Choice or freedom, diversity or disagreement, and scarcity.

HOST B: What does PINC stand for?

HOST A: Power, Ideology, Norms, Communication.

HOST B: A city bans fossil heating in new buildings. Is the new rule polity, politics or policy?

HOST A: Policy. It's the substantive content. The council debate that produced it is politics, and the city's constitution and council rules are polity.

HOST B: Name Patzelt's four challenges for political science.

HOST A: Complexity, historicity, the competing self-knowledge of research subjects, and the researchers' connections with their object of study.

HOST B: And the two halves of climate politics?

HOST A: Mitigation, limiting anthropogenic climate change, and adaptation, dealing with its consequences.

HOST B: Two reflection questions from the course to think about before next time. Where in your everyday life do you actively notice politics? And is a society without politics conceivable? What would happen if politics no longer existed?

HOST A: Next episode: power. Weber, Popitz's four forms of power, Arendt's "power to," and Foucault. See you there.
