# Episode 4 · Institutions

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome to episode four! Today: institutions.

HOST A: The lecture opened with a quote about Iran. The idea: a government can be unpopular with most people and still be really hard to remove, because it's built into everything. Schools, police, banks, the army.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] So institutions can keep things going, even when people don't like them.

HOST A: Exactly. That's today's theme. The reading is by Gerhard Göhler. He says institutions are things built to last, and they make life predictable.

HOST B: Predictable how?

HOST A: Think of traffic rules. You don't have to wonder, every time a car comes towards you, which side it'll pass on. You just know.

HOST B: So what exactly counts as an institution?

HOST A: This is the key part, so remember it. Göhler says three things must be true. [slowly] One: there are rules. Two: the rules are actually used. Three: the people affected know about them.

HOST B: Rules, used, known.

HOST A: Perfect. And the rules can be formal, meaning written down, like laws. Or informal, unwritten, like customs.

HOST B: In class there was a fun test with four examples, right?

HOST A: Yes. Let's play it. Example one: the Swiss climate law, net zero by 2050, approved by voters in 2023. Institution or not?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Yes. Rules, used, known. A formal, political institution.

HOST B: Example two: at UN climate conferences, they never vote. Everything is decided by consensus. But that's not actually written down anywhere.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Still yes! The rule exists, it's always used, and everyone knows it. An informal institution.

HOST B: Example three: a company puts "net zero by 2040" on its website. But inside the company, almost nobody knows, and it changes nothing.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: No. There's a rule, but nobody uses it and nobody knows about it.

HOST B: [laughs] And example four: in some friend groups, people roll their eyes if you fly to Barcelona just for the weekend. Flight shame.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Yes, kind of. It's an informal social institution, but only inside that group.

HOST B: So the lesson is...

HOST A: Being written down doesn't matter. The consensus rule isn't written down but counts. The company target is written down but doesn't count.

HOST B: Love that. Now, why do people follow institutions? Just because they're forced to?

HOST A: Mostly no. Göhler says force only works now and then. Institutions really work when people believe in them. He even quotes Hannah Arendt: institutions decay as soon as people stop supporting them.

HOST B: And institutions do two jobs, right?

HOST A: Yes. They set rules, and they give us values to orient ourselves by. For political institutions specifically, these two jobs are called steering and integration.

HOST A: Steering means making and enforcing decisions for everyone. Encouraging good behaviour, discouraging bad behaviour.

HOST B: And integration?

HOST A: Integration means making people feel they belong. Feeling that the country's values are their values. And that happens through symbols.

HOST B: Like flags?

HOST A: Exactly. Flags, national holidays, like the first of August in Switzerland. Or the dome of the Swiss parliament, which shows the coats of arms of all the cantons together.

HOST B: Pause and answer. What are the two main jobs of political institutions?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Steering, making binding decisions. And integration, making people feel they belong.

HOST B: Okay. Then the lecture gave three different ways to explain institutions.

HOST A: Yes, three lenses. Lens one: institutions as rules of a game. People are rational. They want things, and they use rules to get them.

HOST B: So people follow rules because it pays off.

HOST A: Exactly. Lens two: institutions as culture. People follow rules because that's just "how things are done around here". It feels normal and right, even if it isn't efficient.

HOST A: Lens three: institutions as history. Decisions made long ago keep shaping today. Once you're on a path, it's hard to leave it. That's called path dependence.

HOST B: Can you show all three with one example?

HOST A: Sure. The UN consensus rule. Lens one: every country likes being able to block things, so it protects its own interests. Lens two: consensus just feels like the proper "UN way". Lens three: to change the rule, you'd need... consensus! So you're stuck.

HOST B: [laughs] That's a trap. It's called lock-in, right?

HOST B: Quiz. Which lens says people follow rules because it feels normal and appropriate?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: The culture lens. Sociological institutionalism.

HOST B: Now the big application: from Kyoto to Paris.

HOST A: Yes. Kyoto, 1997. Only rich countries had to cut emissions, by fixed, legally binding amounts. Other countries had no targets. And the US never joined.

HOST B: And Paris, 2015?

HOST A: Very different. A clear goal: keep warming well below two degrees, ideally one point five. Every country sets its own plan, and has to make it more ambitious every five years. And everyone reports on their progress.

HOST B: So which one was better?

HOST A: [laughs] Trick question. Use the two jobs. Kyoto was strong at steering, with hard, binding targets. But weak at integration, because only some countries were in, and the US left.

HOST B: And Paris is the opposite?

HOST A: Exactly. Weak at steering, no binding targets. But strong at integration, because every country is in and owns its own plan.

HOST B: And the three lenses explain how we got there?

HOST A: Yes. Culture lens: science and public pressure made the two-degree goal feel like the "right" target. History lens: everyone learned from Kyoto's failure that the US had to be on board, and the failed Copenhagen summit in 2009 started the "every country sets its own plan" idea. Rules-of-the-game lens: powerful countries had red lines. China refused binding targets from outside, so self-chosen plans were the deal.

HOST B: And the lesson is you need all three lenses.

HOST A: Exactly. One lens alone misses part of the story.

HOST B: There were two practical lessons for climate policy too.

HOST A: Yes. One: lock in climate-friendly paths, so they're hard to undo. Two: break down the institutions that keep polluting paths alive.

HOST B: And a bonus question: why is US climate policy weak?

HOST A: Two explanations. First, interests. The US feels less threatened by climate change, and changing would cost it a lot. A tiny island like Vanuatu is the opposite.

HOST B: And second?

HOST A: The political system. Where politicians are less exposed to election pressure, and where losers like coal workers can be compensated, like Germany or Switzerland, countries tend to be more ambitious. The US has neither.

HOST B: Final quiz! Göhler's three conditions for an institution?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: There are rules, they're used, and people know about them.

HOST B: Paris compared to Kyoto: better at steering or at integration?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Better at integration. Weaker at steering.

HOST B: What's path dependence?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Decisions from the past keep shaping today, so it's hard to switch paths.

HOST B: [warmly] Nice. Next episode is a bit different. It's the first online unit: Hannah Arendt on power and violence, lobbying in the Swiss parliament, and how to write your own idea for future generations.

HOST A: See you there!
