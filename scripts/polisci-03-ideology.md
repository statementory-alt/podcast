# Episode 3 · Ideology

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome to episode three! Today: ideology.

HOST B: [playfully] The word people use when they mean "your opinions are annoying."

HOST A: [laughs] Exactly! And that's actually the first point of the lecture. The word "ideology" is used in three different ways.

HOST B: Okay, give me the three.

HOST A: One: ideology as a stubborn, rigid way of thinking. That's inside one person. "Don't be so ideological." It's an insult.

HOST B: Two?

HOST A: Ideology as a tool of control. That's at the level of society. Ideas that keep powerful people on top. Also negative.

HOST B: And three?

HOST A: Ideology as a set of ideas that guides political action. Like liberalism or socialism. That one is neutral. Not good or bad, just a description.

HOST B: And the lecture focuses on two and three.

HOST A: Exactly. Let's start with number two: ideology as control. The big question here is: why do people accept situations that go against their own interests?

HOST B: [thoughtfully] And how can people who are being controlled still feel free?

HOST A: Yes! To answer that, the lecture uses Steven Lukes. He says power has three faces.

HOST B: Three faces. Okay.

HOST A: Face one: winning decisions. Who wins the vote? That's visible. You can just count.

HOST B: Like a parliament voting on a climate law.

HOST A: Perfect. Face two: setting the agenda. Deciding what even gets discussed. This is harder to see, it happens behind the scenes.

HOST B: Example?

HOST A: For decades, international climate talks barely mentioned fossil fuels. The actual cause of the problem was off the table.

HOST B: [surprised] Wow. That's sneaky.

HOST A: And face three: shaping what people want in the first place. This is almost invisible.

HOST B: Like what?

HOST A: The idea of your personal "carbon footprint". It makes climate change feel like your private shopping problem, instead of a question about big companies and laws.

HOST B: Your turn. The long silence about fossil fuels in climate talks. Which face of power is that?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Face two. Agenda-setting. Deciding what gets talked about.

HOST B: Now Karl Marx. He's the big name for ideology as control, right?

HOST A: Yes. His famous line: the ideas of the ruling class are, in every era, the ruling ideas.

HOST B: So whoever owns the factories also controls what people think?

HOST A: That's the idea. He has a simple model. At the bottom is the economy, who owns what. On top are ideas: laws, politics, religion, art. The economy shapes the ideas, and the ideas protect the economy.

HOST A: Then the philosopher Theodor Adorno went even further. He said people can be shaped so deeply that they don't even want to resist anymore.

HOST B: [skeptically] Hmm. But who decides what people "really" want?

HOST A: [excited] That's exactly the problem the lecture points out! If everyone is fooled, how is the critic the only one who sees clearly? Who gets to say they know your true interests better than you?

HOST B: Good point. Okay, now meaning number three. The neutral one.

HOST A: This comes from Andrew Heywood, who wrote the reading. Here's his definition, worth learning. [slowly] An ideology is a more or less coherent set of ideas that provides the basis for organised political action, whether to keep, change, or overthrow the existing system of power.

HOST A: He says every ideology does three things. First: it describes the world as it is. Second: it paints a picture of the ideal future. And third: it explains how to get from here to there.

HOST B: Where we are, where we want to be, and how to get there.

HOST A: Perfect summary. And ideologies do three jobs for us. They help us make sense of the world. They give politics a moral purpose. And they bring people together into a group.

HOST B: Quick check for the listener. What are the three things every ideology does, according to Heywood?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: Describe the world now, imagine a better future, and explain how to get there.

HOST A: Ideologies have core ideas in the middle, and less important ideas around them. Like furniture in a room. A kitchen has a stove and a fridge. You can add a table, but it's still a kitchen.

HOST A: "Thick" ones like liberalism have lots. "Thin" ones like populism or nationalism have very few, so they attach to other ideologies.

HOST B: Okay, now the three classics. Liberalism first.

HOST A: Liberalism's core is liberty, the individual, and reason. Liberals see society as free individuals with rights. The big danger is too much power in one place. The goal is people living their own lives, protected by fair laws. And change should come step by step.

HOST B: And there are two types of liberals?

HOST A: Yes. Same core, different neighbours. Classical liberals want a small state and free markets. Freedom means being left alone. Social liberals want a welfare state and good schools. Freedom means having the chance to develop yourself.

HOST B: Conservatism.

HOST A: Core: tradition, human imperfection, and society as a living whole. Conservatives think society grew slowly over time, and our reason is limited, so old institutions hold wisdom. Change should be careful. The famous phrase is "change in order to conserve."

HOST B: And socialism.

HOST A: Core: community, equality, solidarity. Socialists think capitalism splits society into classes and creates inequality. The goal is a community of equals. And change comes through collective action, by revolution or by reform.

HOST B: Pause time. Name the three core ideas of liberalism.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Liberty, the individual, and reason.

HOST B: Now the fun part. How does each one see the climate crisis?

HOST A: There were four slogans in the lecture. "Let's create carbon markets!" That's liberal. "Step by step, no radical moves!" That's conservative. "We need a fair transition!" That's socialist. And "We need to change how we think!" That's green, or ecologist.

HOST B: So for liberals, climate change is...

HOST A: A market failure. Polluters harm other people's freedom. Their tool is a carbon tax, with the money given back to people. The tension: it's hard to protect freedom from harm that's spread out, global, and in the future. Their fix: protecting the freedom of future people too, like Germany's top court did in 2021.

HOST B: Conservatives?

HOST A: Climate change threatens things we love, like our landscapes. But some conservatives see climate policy itself as the threat. Remember, Swiss voters rejected the CO2 law in 2021. The tension: to protect what we love, we now need big, fast change, which conservatives don't like. Their fix: taking care of things for our grandchildren.

HOST B: And socialists?

HOST A: For them, climate change is an injustice. The richest ten percent caused about half the emissions between 1990 and 2015, but the poorest suffer most. The yellow vest protests in France showed: no climate policy without fairness. The tension: socialists traditionally love industry and jobs, like coal jobs. Their fix: green socialism, or even less growth.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] And all three have one thing in common?

HOST A: Yes. They're all about humans. Freedom, tradition, equality... those are all relationships between people. Nature is just in the background. Green thinking puts nature at the centre. Ecuador even gave nature legal rights in its constitution in 2008.

HOST B: Which raises the question: who belongs to the political community? Only humans?

HOST B: Final quiz! What are Lukes' three faces of power?

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Winning decisions, setting the agenda, and shaping what people want.

HOST B: For a liberal, what kind of problem is climate change?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: A market failure, and harm to other people's freedom.

HOST B: What do liberalism, conservatism and socialism all have in common when it comes to nature?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: They're all focused on humans. Nature is just in the background.

HOST B: [warmly] Great. Next time: institutions. What they are, why they're so hard to change, and how the world got from Kyoto to the Paris Agreement.

HOST A: See you there!
