# Episode 2 · Power

HOST A: Welcome back. This is episode two, on Lecture 2: Power. The reading is Heinrich Popitz, "The Concept of Power," and we'll also bring in Max Weber, Hannah Arendt and Michel Foucault.

HOST B: The lecture opens with a quote from David Hume, from 1741. "Nothing appears more surprising to those who consider human affairs with a philosophical eye than the easiness with which the many are governed by the few."

HOST A: That's the puzzle of power. How do a few people manage to govern so many, and so easily? Keep that question in your head for the whole episode.

HOST B: Then the classic definition. Max Weber.

HOST A: "Power is the probability that one actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his own will despite resistance, regardless of the basis on which this probability rests." Three things to notice. It's a probability, or a chance, not a certainty. It exists within a social relationship. And it doesn't matter what it rests on. Weber himself says all conceivable qualities of a person and all conceivable circumstances can put someone in a position to impose their will.

HOST B: Now Popitz. He starts with what he calls the historical premises of the problematization of power. Basically: what assumptions does our modern way of thinking about power rest on?

HOST A: He names three. Premise one: power orders are humanly produced. They aren't ordained by God, set by myth, imposed by nature or fixed by sacred tradition. They're made by people, so they can be remade. Popitz calls this perhaps the real "idea of the political." It first appears in the Greek polis, which produced the first comparative theories of constitutions, with Plato and Aristotle. It comes back in the bourgeois revolutions of the modern era. He quotes Alexander Hamilton in the Federalist Papers asking whether societies can establish good government "from reflection and choice," or are forever dependent on "accident and force."

HOST B: Premise two.

HOST A: Power is ubiquitous. With the bourgeois revolutions, power stops being seen as something that belongs only to the state. New classes develop their own power: the educated bourgeoisie with public opinion and the power of ideas, the propertied bourgeoisie with money, and the working class as a counter-power. Even the relationships between men and women and between parents and children come to be seen as power relationships. Popitz calls this the "generalization of the suspicion of power." Power lurks behind everything. Weber's definition reflects that, because power can attach to any kind of relationship.

HOST B: And premise three.

HOST A: Power limits freedom, and therefore it needs justification. Every exercise of power is a limitation of somebody's freedom. That doesn't mean all power is bad. Popitz mentions protective power over children, or the concentrated power needed to secure law and peace. But in modern society every power, in the state or in the family, has to answer the question "why?"

HOST B: There's a self-assessment question on this. Which historical pair does Popitz refer to as shaping the problematization of power? Caesar and Napoleon, Morgarten and Sempach, the founding of the USA and the French Revolution, or the First and Second World Wars?

HOST A: The founding of the USA and the French Revolution. The bourgeois revolutions. He spends more time on the USA, and mentions France briefly.

HOST B: Now the core: the four basic anthropological forms of power. Popitz asks two questions. On what human capacities does power rest? And why do humans have to suffer power? Each form pairs a capacity of the power-holder with a vital dependency of the power-subject.

HOST A: And he illustrates them with a chorus from Sophocles' Antigone, "Wonders are many, and none is more wonderful than man." Let's go through them.

HOST B: Form one.

HOST A: Power of action, Aktionsmacht. The capacity is the ability to act so as to inflict harm on others, Verletzungskraft. The dependency is vulnerability, Verwundbarkeit, our ability to suffer harm. Sophocles' image is the hunter who traps and kills animals. Humans can harm each other's bodies, their economic livelihood, through robbery or cutting off access to land, and their social participation, through exclusion. And because weapons and organisation can boost the capacity to harm without limit, human dangerousness is potentially unlimited. Power of action can be a single act. It doesn't need any ongoing control.

HOST B: Form two.

HOST A: Instrumental power. Sophocles' image is taming the horse and the bull under the yoke. Now power becomes durable. The capacity is the ability to engender fears and hopes, through threats and promises. The dependency is our concern for the future, Zukunftsorientiertheit. Because we orient our action to what we expect will happen, someone who can credibly promise rewards or threaten punishments can steer us. A threat is a form of extortion, a promise a form of corruption. The motives are fear and hope.

HOST B: And Popitz says something about how it works structurally.

HOST A: Yes. The power-holder poses an alternative: submit or don't. Everything the affected person does becomes a yes or a no to a question they didn't ask. Their definition of the situation is imposed on them. Popitz calls instrumental power the typical power of everyday life, and says every long-lasting power relationship also rests on it.

HOST B: That's another self-assessment question, by the way. Which basic form of power is linked to threats and promises, extortion and corruption?

HOST A: Instrumental power.

HOST B: Form three.

HOST A: Authoritative power. Here Popitz distinguishes external power, which works through external advantages and disadvantages, from internal power, which produces a willing disposition to obey. It works even where nobody is watching. "Internal power works even in a dark hole," as he puts it. The capacity is the ability to set standards. The dependency is our need for standards and for recognition, Massstabs- und Anerkennungsbedürftigkeit. Humans need norms to orient their action, and our self-esteem depends on being recognised by the people we accept as standard-setters. So the alternative here is between hoped-for recognition and feared withdrawal of recognition.

HOST B: And crucially, authoritative power guides not just behaviour but attitudes.

HOST A: Exactly. Perspectives, criteria of judgement, how people see and evaluate things. That's what distinguishes it from instrumental power, which only guides conduct.

HOST B: Form four, the one with the strange name.

HOST A: Power of data constitution, datensetzende Macht. Sophocles' image is ploughing the earth. The capacity is technical action, the ability to shape the material, artificial world. The dependency is that we're bound to an artificially modified world of objects. Every artefact adds a new "datum" to the world: the road smooths the way for many, the wall blocks it. Those who design a new settlement determine the conditions of existence for others. Popitz says they "build worlds for others." And it isn't the power of things over people. It's the power of the producer, built into things, which can stay latent for a long time.

HOST B: Popitz also groups them in pairs.

HOST A: Instrumental and authoritative power both steer conduct, through alternatives. Instrumental steers only behaviour, authoritative steers behaviour and attitudes. Power of action and power of data constitution both modify the situation of those affected. Power of action affects the person directly. Data constitution sets the material conditions of existence.

HOST B: And they combine.

HOST A: Very often. Conquest is power of action, the new possessions become sites of instrumental power, oppression can be transfigured into authority, and all of it gets built into walls and fortifications. Popitz speaks of a "tendency of power forms toward reciprocal attraction." His closing example is from Peter Weiss. A boy moves to a new house. The neighbour's son, Friederle, boasts that his father owns the house, tells him his hat says "I am stupid," and throws the hat into a tree. That's power of action, and it sets up instrumental power. But what really traps the boy is his mother, who calls out "Have you found a new playmate already? Are you having fun?" He answers "Yes, we are playing very well," because he doesn't want to disappoint her. Her authoritative power closes off every exit.

HOST B: That's such a good example. Now the in-class application. The lecture hall was split into local, national and international groups, each with four climate cases, and for each one you decide: is there power in Popitz's sense, and which form? One case per set was deliberately contested.

HOST A: Local level first. Case one: Standing Rock, where police cleared protest camps against the Dakota Access Pipeline with water cannons in freezing temperatures and made arrests. That's power of action. Case two: a city closes its centre to private cars and turns the space into pedestrian and cycling paths. Power of data constitution, because it reshapes the physical environment. Case three: a municipality subsidises heat pumps and announces fines for old oil heating. Instrumental power, promises and threats. Case four, the contested one: activists blockade Altenrhein airport before the WEF in Davos. You could read it as power of action, or as authoritative power, because it's about setting standards and getting attention.

HOST B: National level.

HOST A: Case one: before the Swiss CO2 Act referendum, business and petroleum associations warn about rising prices and job cuts. Instrumental power, a threat. Case two: the government bans fossil heating in new buildings. Power of data constitution. Case three: nationwide school climate strikes, after which parties reposition on climate. Authoritative power, shifting standards. Case four, contested: a fossil fuel tax triggers violent road blockades, police clear them, and the government withdraws the tax. You can read power of action, and instrumental power.

HOST B: And international.

HOST A: Case one: the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, CBAM, after which trading partners introduce their own carbon levies to avoid the tariffs. Instrumental power. Case two: an oil pipeline of over a thousand kilometres through two East African countries that locks them into fossil exports for decades. Power of data constitution. Case three: environmental NGOs give out a mock "Fossil of the Day" award at UN climate talks. Authoritative power, through naming and shaming. Case four, contested: Vanuatu leads an initiative for an International Court of Justice advisory opinion on states' climate obligations. Arguably authoritative power too.

HOST B: And there's one empty cell in the solution table: power of action at the international level. Why?

HOST A: That's self-assessment question four, and it's a great exam question. Power of action plays a minor role internationally for two reasons. First, there's no central authority. The international system is anarchic, and at local and national level power of action was tied to the police, meaning central state authority. Second, states haven't used force for climate goals. No state has destroyed another's infrastructure to cut its emissions, the climate isn't a typical cause of war, and the strongest military powers are also among the biggest emitters.

HOST B: But the course also gives alternative readings.

HOST A: Yes. Wars over oil, like US interventions in Iraq or Venezuela, combine force with threats and promises, so in the end that's instrumental power. You could call the appropriation of the atmosphere as a pollution sink a kind of "theft," but that's a borderline case, arguably power of data constitution. And you could exploit economic or financial interdependence as a vulnerability, but that's rare in climate policy.

HOST B: Then the lecture expands the concept of power. Weber, and Popitz really, see power as control. Power over.

HOST A: Power over is based on violence, threats and sanctions, authority, and shaping the environment. It restricts freedom. And it's asymmetrical: B's consent is coerced.

HOST B: And then Hannah Arendt flips it.

HOST A: Arendt, 1970: "Power corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert. Power is never the property of an individual; it belongs to a group and remains in existence only as long as the group keeps together." This is power to. Power as a creative ability.

HOST B: She makes some provocative claims.

HOST A: She says power needs no justification, because it's "inherent in the very existence of political communities." What it does need is legitimacy. And legitimacy comes from "the initial getting together rather than from any action that then may follow." She also insists that violence is not a form of power. They're "distinct phenomena." In her words, violence can always destroy power, and out of the barrel of a gun grows the most effective command, but "what never can grow out of it is power."

HOST B: So power to is based on common action and the possibility to bring something new into the world. It enables freedom, and it's symmetrical: A and B share a common goal.

HOST A: And we'll go much deeper into Arendt in episode five, because DLU 1 is built around her text.

HOST B: Then the third perspective: Michel Foucault.

HOST A: Foucault's key idea: modern subjects are the product of power. Power doesn't just constrain individuals who already exist. It constitutes them. That's the productive, or constitutive, aspect of power. Second, in modern societies, disciplinary power is just as important as sovereign power. The slide showed the panopticon, through the Presidio Modelo prison in Cuba, where inmates can always be watched and so learn to discipline themselves. Third, power works through material and immaterial structures, meaning discourse. So to analyse power, you have to analyse discourses.

HOST B: The synthesis table puts it together.

HOST A: Power over, as control, is based on violence, threats and sanctions, authority, and shaping the environment through material and also immaterial structures, meaning discourse. It's freedom-constraining, including through discourse, it's asymmetrical, and its thinkers are Weber, Popitz and Foucault. Power to, as creative ability, is based on common action and bringing something new into the world. It's freedom-enabling, symmetrical, and its thinker is Arendt.

HOST B: Now the trickiest self-assessment question. Patzelt quotes Weber: power is the chance to impose your will even against resistance, exercised also as the power to prevent and the power to shape the very concepts and ways of thinking. True or false. A, this covers Popitz's authoritative power.

HOST A: True. Shaping concepts and ways of thinking matches authoritative power, which also guides attitudes, perspectives and criteria of judgement.

HOST B: B, it encompasses Arendt's understanding of power.

HOST A: False. Patzelt draws on Weber's power as control. Arendt sees power as a creative ability.

HOST B: C, it's incompatible with Popitz's view that vulnerability is a fundamental condition explaining power.

HOST A: False. Vulnerability underpins power of action, which is exactly about imposing your will against resistance. That fits Weber and Patzelt. The official answer sheet doesn't explain this one, so that reasoning is inferred, but it's consistent.

HOST B: D, it's incompatible with Foucault's thesis that discourses must be examined to analyse power.

HOST A: False. Discourses shape exactly the concepts and ways of thinking Patzelt mentions.

HOST B: Lecture summary.

HOST A: Power is an ever-present companion in social relationships and takes various forms. The useful distinctions are: Popitz's four basic anthropological forms, external versus internal power, Weber's power over versus Arendt's power to, and the often overlooked productive or constitutive side of power from Foucault. And the lecture previewed Steven Lukes' three dimensions of power, decision-making, agenda-setting and preference-shaping, which come up in Lecture 3.

HOST B: Quick quiz. Match the form of power to the dependency. Concern for the future?

HOST A: Instrumental power.

HOST B: Need for standards and recognition?

HOST A: Authoritative power.

HOST B: Vulnerability?

HOST A: Power of action.

HOST B: Dependence on an artificially modified world of objects?

HOST A: Power of data constitution.

HOST B: According to Arendt, does power need justification?

HOST A: No. Power needs legitimacy, which comes from people coming together. Violence can be justified, but never legitimate.
