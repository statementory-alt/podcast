# Episode 2 · Power

HOST A: [warmly] Welcome back! Episode two. Today: power.

HOST B: [playfully] Ooh. The juicy one.

HOST A: [laughs] It really is. The lecture starts with a puzzle from the philosopher David Hume. He wrote that nothing is more surprising than how easily the many are governed by the few.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] Huh. Yeah. Why do millions of people just... go along with a small government?

HOST A: That's the question for today. Let's start with the classic definition, from Max Weber. [slowly] Power is the chance that someone in a relationship can carry out their own will, even against resistance.

HOST B: So basically: can you get your way, even if others push back?

HOST A: Exactly. And it doesn't matter where that power comes from. Money, muscles, charm, a job title... anything.

HOST B: Okay. And then the reading is by Heinrich Popitz.

HOST A: Right. Popitz says our modern way of thinking about power rests on three big ideas.

HOST B: Go.

HOST A: One: power is made by people. It isn't given by God or nature. So it can be changed.

HOST A: Two: power is everywhere. Not just in government, but in families, friendships, workplaces.

HOST A: And three: power limits freedom, so it always has to justify itself. Anyone with power has to answer the question "why?"

HOST B: And where do these ideas come from historically?

HOST A: Popitz points to the big revolutions of the late 1700s. The founding of the USA, and the French Revolution. That's an actual exam question, by the way.

HOST B: Let's test it. Which historical events does Popitz link to our modern idea of power?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: The founding of the USA and the French Revolution.

HOST B: Now the main part. Popitz's four kinds of power.

HOST A: Yes, and here's the trick to remembering them. Each kind of power matches a weakness that all humans have.

HOST B: Oh, I like that. Power works because we're vulnerable somehow.

HOST A: Exactly. Number one: power of action. That's the ability to hurt someone.

HOST B: And the weakness is... we can get hurt.

HOST A: Right. Our bodies, our money, our place in a group. Think of police clearing a protest camp.

HOST B: Number two?

HOST A: Instrumental power. Threats and promises. "Do this, or else." Or "do this, and you'll get a reward."

HOST B: And the weakness?

HOST A: We worry about the future. We fear punishment and hope for rewards. Popitz says this is the most everyday kind of power there is.

HOST A: Number three: authoritative power. This is the power to set standards. To decide what's good, normal, or right.

HOST B: And the weakness is that we want approval?

HOST A: Exactly. We need standards to live by, and we want recognition from people we look up to. And the special thing here: this kind of power changes not just what we do, but what we think.

HOST B: So it works even when nobody's watching.

HOST A: Yes. Popitz says it works even "in a dark hole". Number four, with the weirdest name: power of data constitution.

HOST A: It just means power through building things. Roads, walls, buildings, pipelines. When you build something, you shape everyone else's life around it.

HOST B: And the weakness is that we all live in a world other people built.

HOST A: Exactly. A road makes some trips easy. A wall blocks them.

HOST B: Okay, quiz time. Match the power to the example. A bank says: cut your emissions or we stop your loan. Which kind of power?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Instrumental power. It's a threat.

HOST B: A city turns car lanes into bike paths.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Power of data constitution. It changes the physical world.

HOST B: A famous scientist convinces people that flying is irresponsible.

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Authoritative power. It sets a standard and changes what people think.

HOST B: In class, everyone did this with real climate examples, right?

HOST A: Yes. Some quick ones. Police clearing the Standing Rock protest camps: power of action. A town giving money for heat pumps and fining old oil heaters: instrumental power. School climate strikes changing what parties stand for: authoritative power. And a huge oil pipeline that locks countries into oil for decades: power of data constitution.

HOST B: And there was one empty box in the table.

HOST A: Right. Power of action at the international level. No examples.

HOST B: Why not?

HOST A: Two reasons. First, there's no world police, so nobody can physically force countries. Second, countries just don't go to war over climate. The biggest militaries are also some of the biggest polluters.

HOST A: Now the lecture zooms out. Everything so far is "power over". One side controls the other.

HOST B: And then Hannah Arendt comes in with something different.

HOST A: Yes. Arendt says real power is "power to". [slowly] Power is the human ability not just to act, but to act together. It never belongs to one person. It belongs to a group, and only lasts as long as the group sticks together.

HOST B: So a climate strike with thousands of students... that's power, in Arendt's sense.

HOST A: Exactly. And she says something surprising: violence is not power. They're actually opposites.

HOST B: Wait, really? A gun seems pretty powerful.

HOST A: [laughs] Her famous line: violence can always destroy power. A gun gets you instant obedience. But it can never create power, because power comes from people choosing to act together.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] So you can force people to obey, but you can't force them to really support you.

HOST A: Beautifully put. And we'll go deeper into Arendt in episode five.

HOST B: Then the third thinker: Michel Foucault.

HOST A: Foucault says power doesn't just push people around. It actually shapes who we are. Our habits, what we think is normal, even how we see ourselves.

HOST A: He also talks about discipline. The famous image is a prison where guards might always be watching, so prisoners start controlling themselves.

HOST A: And his last point: power works through what we say and how we talk about things. So if you want to understand power, look at the language and the ideas people use.

HOST B: Okay, can you sum up the big contrast?

HOST A: Sure. "Power over" means control. It's based on force, threats, authority, or building the world. It limits freedom, and one side is on top. That's Weber, Popitz and Foucault. "Power to" means acting together. It creates freedom, and people are equals. That's Arendt.

HOST B: Final quiz! According to Arendt, does power belong to one person or to a group?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: To a group. And only while the group sticks together.

HOST B: Name Popitz's four kinds of power.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Power of action, instrumental power, authoritative power, and power of data constitution.

HOST B: Which one is linked to threats and promises?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Instrumental power.

HOST B: And why is there no international example of power of action?

PAUSE: 7

HOST A: No world police, and countries don't use force over climate.

HOST B: [warmly] Nice.

HOST A: Next time: ideology. What it means, how ideas can be used to control people, and how liberals, conservatives and socialists each see the climate crisis.

HOST B: See you there!
