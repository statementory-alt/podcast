# Episode 3 · Ideology

HOST A: Welcome to episode three: ideology. This is Lecture 3, and the reading is two chapters of Andrew Heywood's Political Ideologies: "Understanding Political Ideologies" and "Why Political Ideologies Matter."

HOST B: Heywood opens with a nice line. All people are political thinkers, whether they know it or not. We use words like freedom, fairness, equality, liberal or fascist all the time, but rarely precisely.

HOST A: And "ideology" is the worst offender. Heywood quotes David McLellan: ideology is "the most elusive concept in the whole of the social sciences." So the lecture starts by sorting out three different uses of the word.

HOST B: Use number one: ideology as a particularly rigid thinking pattern. It sits in the individual, and it's pejorative. Think of calling someone "so ideological."

HOST A: Use number two: ideology as an instrument of domination. It sits in society, and it's pejorative in a critical sense. That's the Marxist and critical theory tradition.

HOST B: And use number three: ideology as an action-oriented paradigm. It sits in political competition, and it's neutral, analytical. That's Heywood's use.

HOST A: The lecture focuses on the second and third. The first, the psychology of rigid thinking, is explored in Leor Zmigrod's book The Ideological Brain, which is in the further resources.

HOST B: Quick history first, from Heywood. Where does the word come from?

HOST A: It was coined during the French Revolution by Antoine Destutt de Tracy and first used in public in 1796. For him, idéologie was a new "science of ideas," literally idea-ology, which he hoped would become the queen of the sciences. That original meaning had almost no influence afterwards. What shaped the term was Marx.

HOST B: So let's do use number two: ideology as an instrument of domination. The critical theory tradition asks two questions. Why do people accept social and political conditions that go against their own objective interests? And how can people who are subject to domination still feel free?

HOST A: Before Marx, the lecture brings in Steven Lukes' three dimensions of power, which Lecture 2 previewed. This table is very exam-relevant.

HOST B: First dimension.

HOST A: Power as the ability to succeed in decision-making. The focus is the formal political arena. The method is counting votes and decisions in decision-making forums. Power is visible and transparent. The climate example is a vote on a climate law in parliament.

HOST B: Second dimension.

HOST A: Power as the ability to set the agenda, also called non-decision power. The focus is the formal arena plus informal processes, the "corridors of power." The method is an ethnography of those corridors, to show how the agenda is set. Power here is invisible to the public, but it can be made visible. The climate example: the long absence of fossil fuels from international climate negotiations. For decades, the actual source of the problem wasn't even on the table.

HOST B: Third dimension.

HOST A: Power as the ability to shape preferences. The focus is civil society in general, especially the public sphere where preferences are formed. The method is ideology critique: showing how actors come to misperceive their own material interests. Power is largely invisible and has to be "demystified." The climate example: the idea of the personal CO2 footprint, which turns climate protection into a question of individual consumption rather than, say, regulation of producers.

HOST B: Decision-making, agenda-setting, preference-shaping. Visible, hideable, invisible.

HOST A: Now Marx. The key quote, from The German Ideology with Engels: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas." The class that controls material production also controls mental production. And in the Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, ideology includes the dominant "legal, political, religious, aesthetic or philosophic" elements of society.

HOST B: And the base-superstructure model.

HOST A: The material basis, meaning economic relations, shapes the ideational superstructure. And the superstructure in turn legitimises and maintains the material basis. So it's mutual shaping and maintaining.

HOST B: Heywood lists four features of Marx's concept.

HOST A: One, ideology is about delusion and mystification. It produces a false picture of the world, what Engels later called "false consciousness." Marx used the image of the camera obscura, where the picture comes out upside down. Two, it's linked to class. It reflects the interests of the ruling class, so capitalist ideology is bourgeois ideology, and liberalism, which presents rights only the propertied can use as universal, is the classic example. Three, ideology is a manifestation of power, because it hides exploitation from the proletariat. Four, it's temporary. It will disappear when class society disappears.

HOST B: And later Marxists changed it.

HOST A: Lenin and later Marxists treated ideology as the distinctive ideas of any class, including the working class, which made the term less negative. Antonio Gramsci developed the idea of hegemony: capitalism survives not only through economic and political power, but because bourgeois ideas become the common sense of the age, embedded in art, education, media, language and popular culture. And the Frankfurt School, especially Herbert Marcuse in One-Dimensional Man, argued that even liberal tolerance can serve a repressive purpose, by creating the impression of free debate while hiding indoctrination.

HOST B: The lecture's second thinker in this tradition is Theodor Adorno.

HOST A: Adorno's point, via Jaeggi and Celikates: subjects are shaped so deeply, down to their psychological and physical impulses, that no resistance to domination stirs in them any longer.

HOST B: And then the lecture asks the hard question.

HOST A: The problem with the critical concept: how is it possible for the analyst, and for the analyst alone, to see through the fog of the "comprehensive context of delusion," the Verblendungszusammenhang? In other words, who gets to claim they know people's true interests better than the people themselves?

HOST B: Heywood mentions some other non-Marxist views too. Karl Mannheim.

HOST A: Mannheim distinguished ideologies, which defend the existing order and the dominant group's interests, from utopias, idealised pictures of the future that call for radical change and serve subordinate groups. He also distinguished particular ideologies, the ideas of specific people or parties, from total ideologies, the whole world-view of a class or era. He thought every ideological system is distorted, but that a "socially unattached intelligentsia" could still reach objectivity.

HOST B: Then the Cold War liberals.

HOST A: Thinkers like Karl Popper, Hannah Arendt and Bernard Crick used "ideology" restrictively, for closed systems like fascism and communism that claim a monopoly of truth and act like secular religions. By that standard, liberalism, as an open system, isn't an ideology.

HOST B: And conservatives.

HOST A: Conservatives, especially Michael Oakeshott, distrust abstract principles. In politics, he says, "men sail a boundless and bottomless sea." Ideologies try to explain the incomprehensible, so they simplify and distort. Conservatives prefer pragmatism, experience and history.

HOST B: Which brings us to use number three, the neutral one. From the 1960s on, ideology became a neutral, social-scientific concept. Martin Seliger, in 1976, defined it as a set of ideas by which people posit, explain and justify the ends and means of organised social action, whether that action aims to preserve, amend, uproot or rebuild a social order.

HOST A: And that's the basis for Heywood's definition, which you need to know: "An ideology is a more or less coherent set of ideas that provides the basis for organized political action, whether this is intended to preserve, modify or overthrow the existing system of power."

HOST B: And every ideology has three features.

HOST A: A, it advances an account of the existing order, usually as a world-view. B, it outlines a model of the desired future, a vision of the good society. C, it explains how political change can and should be brought about, how to get from A to B.

HOST B: The lecture links this back to Lecture 1. Politics is needed because of choice, disagreement and scarcity. But what do people disagree about? Ideologies structure that disagreement.

HOST A: Heywood also says how ideologies differ from political theory, in three ways. First, instead of focusing on single concepts or great thinkers, ideologies offer contrasting overall perspectives, frameworks or paradigms. Second, ideologies blur the line between descriptive and normative, between what is and what ought to be. Third, ideologies are action-oriented. They're applied political philosophies.

HOST B: He also says ideologies are only "more or less" coherent.

HOST A: Yes. They overlap, they shade into each other, they produce hybrids like conservative nationalism or socialist feminism, and fights within an ideology can be more bitter than fights between them, because people argue about what "true" socialism or "true" liberalism is. Terms like freedom, democracy and justice are what W. B. Gallie called "essentially contested concepts," which can never have one agreed definition.

HOST B: Then there are the three functions of ideologies.

HOST A: Making sense of the world. Investing politics with moral purpose. And forging the collective, meaning group identity and cohesion.

HOST B: And the morphology. This comes from Michael Freeden.

HOST A: Each ideology has core concepts, surrounded by adjacent and peripheral concepts. Heywood's analogy is furniture. The arrangement of furniture tells you whether a room is a kitchen or a bedroom, and a kitchen stays a kitchen even if you add an open-plan dining area. Not every concept has to be present for a doctrine to belong to an ideology. "Thick" ideologies have a full, wide-ranging set of concepts. "Thin-centred" ideologies, like populism or nationalism, have a small core and attach themselves to other "host" ideologies.

HOST B: And there's the distinction between conventional and cross-cutting ideologies.

HOST A: The conventional, classical ideologies are liberalism, conservatism and socialism. Heywood's chapter also lists nationalism, anarchism and possibly fascism as classical. The "new" ideologies include feminism, green ideology or ecologism, multiculturalism, fundamentalism, possibly populism, and postcolonialism. They cut across the traditional left-right spectrum.

HOST B: What changed with the new ones?

HOST A: Heywood names three shifts. From economics to culture: new ideologies care more about values and ways of life than economic organisation, partly because the gap between capitalism and socialism has narrowed. From social politics to identity politics, so political activism becomes almost a lifestyle choice. And greater critical scrutiny of classical ideologies through "decolonising the curriculum," for example the Rhodes Must Fall protests that began in Cape Town in 2015, and the reassessment of Kant's views on colonialism and race.

HOST B: Let's do left and right quickly.

HOST A: The terms come from the seating at the French Estates-General in 1789: radicals on the left, aristocrats on the right. The linear spectrum runs communism, socialism, liberalism, conservatism, fascism. Different criteria have been used: attitudes to change, attitudes to the state and the market, and, for Norberto Bobbio, attitudes to equality, which he sees as the fundamental difference. The left wants more equality, the right sees it as impossible or undesirable.

HOST B: But the linear spectrum has problems.

HOST A: Anarchism can be ultra-left or ultra-right. Some argue the extremes resemble each other, giving the horseshoe spectrum. Populism is right-wing in Europe but historically left-leaning in Latin America. So Hans Eysenck added a second axis, liberty versus authority. And today some see a new divide between "open" and "closed" attitudes, toward globalisation, cultural diversity and national identity.

HOST B: And the "end of ideology" debate.

HOST A: Daniel Bell, in The End of Ideology in 1960, argued that post-war Western politics had become a technical question of how to deliver prosperity. Heywood's reply: that wasn't the end of ideology, it was a consensus on one ideology, welfare capitalism, and it didn't last. The New Left, the New Right and the new ideologies all came after. Francis Fukuyama, in "The End of History" in 1989, argued Western liberal democracy had won. But nationalism, ethnic conflict and religious fundamentalism soon showed otherwise.

HOST B: Now the application: liberalism, conservatism and socialism. Let's take each with its core, world-view, good society and theory of change.

HOST A: Liberalism. Core concepts: liberty, the individual, reason. Adjacent and peripheral: limited power, rights, property, markets, toleration, free trade, the welfare state. World-view: society consists of individuals endowed with reason and rights, and the main threat is concentrated, arbitrary power. Good society: individuals pursue their own idea of the good life, protected by constitutional government and toleration. Change: gradual reform through law, rights, markets and education. Progress is possible.

HOST B: And the lecture shows two liberalisms with the same core but different neighbours.

HOST A: Classical liberalism puts property, the market, a minimal state, free trade and low taxes next to the core. Liberty means freedom from interference, negative liberty, and the state is a night-watchman. Social or modern liberalism puts equal opportunity, the welfare state, social insurance and public education next to the core. Liberty means freedom to develop oneself, positive liberty, and the state is an enabler.

HOST B: Conservatism.

HOST A: Core: tradition, imperfection, organic society. Adjacent: authority, hierarchy, property, community, nation, family, religion, monarchy, free markets. World-view: society is an organic whole that has grown over time, human reason is limited, and inherited institutions store experience nobody could design. Good society: a stable, ordered society held together by authority, duty and bonds across generations, which is Burke. Change: cautious and gradual, "change in order to conserve." Blueprints and radical change are rejected.

HOST B: Socialism.

HOST A: Core: community, equality, solidarity. Adjacent: social class, common ownership, nationalisation, planning, welfare policies. World-view: humans are social, cooperative beings, and capitalism divides society into classes and produces inequality. Good society: a community of equals where everyone's needs are met and solidarity replaces competition. Change: collective action, through revolution or gradual reform.

HOST B: Now the climate crisis. The lecture had four slogans. "Let's create carbon markets!" is liberal. "Radicalism won't get us far, step by step!" is conservative. "What we need is a just transition!" is socialist. And "Our way of thinking needs to change!" is ecologist.

HOST A: For liberalism, climate change is a market failure, and harm to the liberty of others. The typical tool is a CO2 levy: the state sets a price signal, the revenue goes back to the population, and individuals stay free to choose. The tension is liberty versus limits. The harm principle struggles with harm that's diffuse, global and in the future, and state neutrality clashes with the need to change lifestyles. The reconfiguration: extending liberty over time, like the German Constitutional Court's climate ruling in 2021, which protected the freedom of future generations.

HOST B: Conservatism.

HOST A: For conservatism, climate change is a threat to what we cherish. Or, for some conservatives, climate policy itself is the threat. On one side you have Burke's partnership between the living, the dead and those yet to be born. On the other you have conservative opposition to the Swiss CO2 Act, which voters rejected in 2021. The tension is preservation versus gradual change. To preserve, you now need rapid and far-reaching change. There's also experience versus scientific forecasts, and local roots versus a global problem. The reconfiguration: stewardship across generations, and the local community as a source of motivation.

HOST B: Socialism.

HOST A: For socialism, climate change is an injustice produced by capitalism. Those who emit least suffer most. Oxfam found in 2020 that the richest ten percent caused about half of emissions between 1990 and 2015. The Gilets jaunes in France in 2018 showed there's no climate policy without social justice, which leads to the idea of a "just transition." The tension is equality, growth and jobs versus ecology. The socialist tradition is productivist, linking progress to industry and growth, and unions defend coal jobs. The reconfiguration: eco-socialism, sufficiency and degrowth, where growth moves to the periphery or drops out.

HOST B: And then the big question: where is nature?

HOST A: All three are anthropocentric. Liberty, tradition and equality are relations between humans. Nature appears, if at all, at the periphery, as a resource or a backdrop. Ecologism puts nature at the core, for example the rights of nature in Ecuador's constitution of 2008. And that raises a polity question: who belongs to the political community?

HOST B: Heywood's closing chapter asks why ideologies matter. Good side and bad side.

HOST A: The good side is the three functions. Ideologies make sense of the world. They work like paradigms in Thomas Kuhn's sense, lenses that show us what to look at. That also means rival ideologies are "incommensurable." They see different worlds and talk past each other, so debate alone can't settle them. They invest politics with moral purpose, and a politics without ideology would be politics without hope or vision. Heywood suggests this partly explains the rise of populism after 2007 to 2009. And they forge the collective, giving people something bigger than themselves.

HOST B: And the dangers.

HOST A: Imprisoning the mind: tunnel vision, reinforced by sunk cost fallacy, confirmation bias and cognitive entrenchment. Distorting truth: ideologies embody values that can't be scientifically proven, and Foucault called them "regimes of truth," always linked to power. Heywood says populism has made "truth decay" more prominent. And pitting "us" against "them": building group belonging by creating a distrusted "other," which shows up today in culture wars.

HOST B: Lecture key points.

HOST A: Political ideologies shape political views, attitudes and behaviour. Organising majorities often means accommodating some people with different views. Ideologies differ in their diagnoses, their normative goals and their theories of change. Ideologies are less coherent and more full of tension than people think. The climate crisis poses different challenges for different ideologies, and they all share one common challenge: anthropocentrism.

HOST B: Quiz time. Name Lukes' three dimensions of power with a climate example each.

HOST A: Decision-making, a parliamentary vote on a climate law. Agenda-setting, fossil fuels left out of climate negotiations. Preference-shaping, the CO2 footprint making climate an individual consumption issue.

HOST B: What are the three features every ideology has, according to Heywood?

HOST A: An account of the existing order, a model of the good society, and a theory of how to get from one to the other.

HOST B: What's the difference between classical and social liberalism?

HOST A: Same core of liberty, individual and reason. Classical liberalism pairs it with the market and a minimal night-watchman state, and negative liberty. Social liberalism pairs it with the welfare state and equal opportunity, positive liberty, and the state as enabler.

HOST B: And what's the main objection to the critical, Marxist concept of ideology?

HOST A: It's unclear how the analyst alone can see through the "context of delusion" and claim to know people's real interests better than they do.
