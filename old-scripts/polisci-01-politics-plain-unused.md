# Episode 1 · What Is Politics?

HOST A: [warmly] Hey, welcome to the Political Science A study podcast! Episode one. Today's big question: what is politics?

HOST B: [playfully] Sounds easy. Which probably means it isn't.

HOST A: [laughs] We'll keep it simple. Quick tip before we start: when you hear a question, try to answer it out loud. We'll give you a few seconds of silence to do that.

HOST B: Love it. Okay, first, how does the course work?

HOST A: Twelve weeks. Nine normal lectures, and three online units called DLUs. There's a reading before each lecture.

HOST B: And the exam?

HOST A: [seriously] Closed book, on your laptop, two hours, on the fourth of February. It covers the readings, the lectures, and the DLUs. So yes... do the readings.

HOST B: [laughs] Noted. And there's one question that keeps coming back all term, right?

HOST A: Yes. [slowly] Which new political idea would best protect the interests of future generations in climate politics?

HOST B: Future generations. So... people who can't vote, because they're not born yet.

HOST A: Exactly. That's the puzzle. And that's why every lecture uses climate politics as its example.

HOST B: Okay. Why do we even need politics?

HOST A: Three reasons. [pause] First, we have choices. If everything was decided by nature, there'd be nothing to argue about.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: Second, people disagree. We want different things.

HOST B: [laughs] Have you met my flatmates?

HOST A: [laughs] And third, things are scarce. There isn't enough money, land, or time for everyone, so someone has to decide who gets what.

HOST B: Choices, disagreement, scarcity. Easy.

HOST A: Now the most useful tool in the whole course. I call it the three P's. Polity... politics... policy.

HOST B: [curious] They sound almost the same.

HOST A: They do, so here's a simple picture. Think of a football match. Polity is the rules and the stadium. The structure.

HOST B: Okay.

HOST A: Politics is the match itself. The players pushing, arguing, trying to win.

HOST B: And policy?

HOST A: Policy is the result. What actually gets decided. A new tax, a law, a target.

HOST B: [thoughtfully] So if a city bans oil heating in new houses... the ban is policy. The argument in the city council is politics. And the council itself, with its rules, is polity.

HOST A: [impressed] Perfect. That's exactly how you'd answer it in the exam.

HOST B: Let me try one on our listener. Your turn. A country holds a referendum on a climate law. Which part is politics?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: The campaign and the vote, the process. The law itself would be policy, and the referendum rules are polity.

HOST B: Nice. Now, the definitions. Which ones do I need to learn?

HOST A: Two. The first one is short. [slowly] Politics is "the activity through which people make, preserve and amend the general rules under which they live."

HOST B: So: making rules, keeping rules, changing rules.

HOST A: Exactly. The second one is from Werner Patzelt, who wrote the first reading. [slowly] Politics is "human action aimed at establishing and enforcing generally binding rules and decisions within and between groups of people."

HOST B: [laughs] That's a mouthful.

HOST A: So let's break it down. It's done by humans. It's "aimed at" making rules, so it doesn't have to succeed. The rules apply to everyone in the group. And it happens in any group, not just in governments.

HOST B: Can I quiz people with the official question?

HOST A: Go for it.

HOST B: Which of these is politics? A: bees sharing work in a hive. B: wearing a "There is no Planet B" T-shirt in public. C: wearing the same T-shirt at home, alone. D: a flat meeting about the cleaning plan that ends with no decision.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: B and D are politics. A and C are not.

HOST B: Why?

HOST A: Bees aren't humans. The T-shirt at home reaches nobody. But wearing it in public is pushing for a rule for everyone. And the flat meeting tries to make a rule for the group, even if it fails.

HOST B: [excited] So "aimed at" and "any group" are the key words.

HOST A: Exactly. Now Patzelt gives us a little toolkit for looking at anything political. It's called PINC.

HOST B: P, I, N, C.

HOST A: Power. Ideology. Norms. Communication.

HOST B: Explain them like I'm tired.

HOST A: [laughs] Power: who gets their way, even when others resist. Ideology: what people believe about how the world works, even if they're wrong. Norms: the written and unwritten rules people follow. Communication: how people talk to each other, the glue of society.

HOST B: There's a trick question on ideology, right?

HOST A: Yes. For Patzelt, ideology is people's "perceptual reality". Basically, the world as they see it. And people act on that picture, even if it's not true.

HOST B: Quick check. What does the "I" in PINC stand for, and what does it mean for Patzelt?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Ideology. The world as people believe it works, their perceptual reality.

HOST B: Now, climate politics. How does the course define it?

HOST A: Simple. It's politics about climate rules. Rules that try to slow climate change, that's called mitigation. And rules that help us deal with its effects, like floods and heat waves. That's called adaptation.

HOST B: Mitigation: stop it getting worse. Adaptation: live with it.

HOST A: Perfect. And the lecture asked four big questions. What rules can handle the risks? Who pays? Who benefits? And who decides how the rules are made?

HOST B: [interrupting] And that last one is the future-generations problem!

HOST A: [laughs] Exactly. They don't get a seat at the table.

HOST B: Second half of the reading. Why is political science a "science"?

HOST A: Patzelt's idea is nice and humble. Science just has to be better than common sense. Not perfect, just better.

HOST B: What's wrong with common sense?

HOST A: Three things. We only see part of the picture. We see it from our own position. And we think our view is obviously right.

HOST B: [laughs] So when facts don't fit, we doubt the facts, not ourselves.

HOST A: Exactly. He also warns about two classic mistakes. One: judging everyone from a few people you know. Like "my friends hate this law, so everyone does."

HOST B: And the other?

HOST A: The opposite. Assuming every single person in a group is like the group average.

HOST B: Okay. And why is studying politics extra hard? There were four reasons.

HOST A: [slowly] One, it's complex. Lots of people, lots of opinions. Two, it keeps changing, so there are no fixed "laws of history". Three, the people you study already have their own opinions about politics, so your findings either sound obvious or sound wrong.

HOST B: [laughs] Can't win.

HOST A: And four, political scientists are part of the society they study. Their research can even change what happens, like an election poll that changes how people vote.

HOST B: Pause and try this one. Name the four reasons political science is hard.

PAUSE: 8

HOST A: Complexity. Constant change. People already having their own views. And researchers being part of what they study.

HOST B: Last official question. Sandra loves her political party. She starts studying political science. What's hard for her, and what does she gain?

HOST A: Hard: she starts doubting arguments she used to love, maybe even her own party. Patzelt calls it a creative crisis.

HOST B: And the gain?

HOST A: She understands other parties better, and sees the bigger picture behind the news. She starts saying things like "there are at least three sides to this."

HOST B: [laughs] Which is also great exam technique.

HOST A: So, the four big takeaways from Lecture 1. Political science looks at things from many angles. Politics is much more than what politicians do. Always separate polity, politics and policy. And climate politics is connected to everything else.

HOST B: Final quiz! Answer before we do. What are the three reasons we need politics?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Choices, disagreement, and scarcity.

HOST B: What's the difference between mitigation and adaptation?

PAUSE: 6

HOST A: Mitigation slows climate change down. Adaptation helps us live with its effects.

HOST B: And a new carbon tax: polity, politics or policy?

PAUSE: 5

HOST A: Policy. It's the result.

HOST B: [warmly] Nice work. Next time: power. Who has it, where it comes from, and why Hannah Arendt says violence isn't power at all.

HOST A: See you there!
