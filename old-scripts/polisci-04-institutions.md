# Episode 4 · Institutions

HOST A: Welcome to episode four: institutions. This is Lecture 4, and the reading is Gerhard Göhler's chapter "Institution," from 2011.

HOST B: The lecture opens with a quote from Thomas Friedman, from March 2026, about Iran. He asks what happens if a regime is so embedded, in mayoralties, schools, police stations, government jobs, the banking system, the military and neighbourhood militias, that despite being unpopular with most Iranians, it can't be removed without plunging a country of ninety million people into chaos.

HOST A: That's the point of the whole lecture in one sentence. Institutions matter. They can outlast the support of the people who live under them, at least for a while.

HOST B: There was also a painting on an early slide: Ulysses and the Sirens. Ulysses has himself tied to the mast so he can't steer towards the Sirens' song.

HOST A: The slide doesn't explain it, so this is our reading, but it fits the lecture's conclusion perfectly: institutions can bind our future selves. Hold on to that image for the end of the episode.

HOST B: So, what is an institution? Göhler starts with stability.

HOST A: Institutions are social constructs designed for the long term. They're stable in two senses. They are themselves stable, changing more slowly than the social conditions they structure. And they have a stabilising effect, because they make other people's behaviour predictable. His examples: traffic rules mean you don't have to test, every time a car comes towards you, which side it'll pass on. And the modern state, with Weber's monopoly on legitimate physical coercion, means you don't have to think about drawing a weapon every time you meet someone.

HOST B: And then the three conditions, which you absolutely need for the exam.

HOST A: A rule alone isn't an institution. An institution only exists when three conditions are met. One, rules exist. Two, they are applied. Three, the people affected are aware of this. Rules, application, awareness.

HOST B: And they can be formal or informal.

HOST A: Formal rules are codified, like laws, and they're mostly linked to political institutions. Informal rules, the "unwritten laws" based on conventions, customs and traditions, can be just as effective. Göhler's example is the family. Informal institutions have their own sanctions, like social exclusion, which can be even more serious than breaking a formal rule.

HOST B: The lecture tested this with four cases. You decide: rules, applied, aware, and is it an institution?

HOST A: Case one: the Swiss Climate and Innovation Act, net zero by 2050, approved by voters in 2023. Rules, yes. Applied, yes. Aware, yes. It's an institution, formal and political.

HOST B: Case two: UN climate conferences never vote. Decisions are taken by consensus, although that isn't formally written down anywhere.

HOST A: Rules, applied, aware: all yes. It's an institution, informal but political.

HOST B: Case three: a company announces "net zero by 2040" on its website, but internally hardly anyone knows about it and it has no effect on decisions.

HOST A: There's a rule, but it isn't applied and people aren't aware of it. So it's not an institution, even though it's written down.

HOST B: Case four: in some friend groups, people frown on anyone who flies to Barcelona for the weekend. Flight shame.

HOST A: Rules, applied, and aware within the group. So yes, an institution, but with limits. It's informal and social, not political.

HOST B: And the lesson from cases two and three together?

HOST A: Being written down is neither necessary, as case two shows, nor sufficient, as case three shows.

HOST B: Göhler also says coercion alone doesn't make institutions work.

HOST A: Right. Compliance requires external coercion only in rare cases. Institutions get their force when people internalise them. Every coercive regime sets off mechanisms to get around the coercion. And he quotes Hannah Arendt: political institutions "petrify and decay as soon as the living power of the people ceases to uphold them." That's a direct link to episode two and episode five.

HOST B: Then two general functions: regulation and orientation.

HOST A: Regulation means rules telling you what's required and what's forbidden, with sanctions. Orientation means institutions also express values, ideas of a good way of life. The family stands for solidarity from kinship, unions stand for solidarity among workers, and political institutions express a society's fundamental values. They're like signposts. So Göhler's definition is: "social institutions are relatively permanent patterns of behavior and frameworks of meaning, consolidated through internalization, that serve a regulatory and orienting function."

HOST B: And political institutions are a special case.

HOST A: For political institutions, the two functions become steering and integration. Steering, or governance, is about creating and implementing binding decisions for the community, encouraging desired behaviour and discouraging undesired behaviour through incentives and sanctions. Göhler notes that the 1970s dream of direct control through planning was an illusion. Today steering often works indirectly, or even horizontally, which he calls "soft control."

HOST B: And integration.

HOST A: Normative integration. Citizens need to identify with the community's fundamental values, to get a minimum of collective identity. Binding decisions are only seen as legitimate if they're made in the name of the citizens. And that's expressed through symbols and symbolic actions. So Göhler's definition of political institutions is: "regulatory systems for the creation and implementation of binding decisions and instances of the symbolic representation of a society's orientation."

HOST B: The lecture gave three ways to picture integration.

HOST A: First, the normative aspiration: citizens should identify with the community's values and feel they belong. Examples: the UN Charter's preamble, "We the peoples," and the Swiss constitution's preamble, which says the strength of a people is measured by the well-being of its weakest members. Second, the emotional side: citizens see themselves reflected in shared values, through national holidays like the first of August, the Fourth of July or the fourteenth of July, and speeches by heads of state. Third, symbolic representation: institutions make the invisible visible. The dome of the Federal Palace in Bern shows the cantonal coats of arms and so symbolises the Confederation. The same goes for the US flag or the EU flag.

HOST B: There's also a distinction between institutions with and without actors.

HOST A: From Maurice Hauriou. Institutions-personnes are organised bodies defined by the people acting in them: parliament, government, courts. Institutions-choses are systems of norms, independent of individuals, like a constitution.

HOST B: Göhler also insists on the addressees.

HOST A: Yes. Political institutions can't be understood without the citizens, both as addressees, who pay taxes or receive benefits, and as actors. That leads to his model of the institutional configuration.

HOST B: Walk us through it.

HOST A: In a democracy, because of popular sovereignty, citizens are both addressees and actors, and institutions are both actors and addressees. Two relationships connect them: power and representation. Institutions exercise power over citizens. When that power is exercised within an institutional framework and can claim legitimacy, it becomes rule, Herrschaft. That's the steering function. Citizens exercise power too, through personal influence, public opinion, interest groups, citizens' initiatives and elections. And in modern democracies, citizens' power is linked to representation.

HOST B: And representation means?

HOST A: Making something absent present, making the invisible visible, like the will of the citizens, which isn't fixed in advance. Political representation turns citizens' power into accepted decisions. That's the decision-making side. And symbolic representation is the integration side: institutions symbolically express the values and procedures the community is built on. Göhler's example is the German Federal President, who has little real power but uses public speeches to bring shared values to the fore and build trust.

HOST B: There's a footnote on two kinds of power too.

HOST A: Transitive power is Weber's power over others, which flows from institutions to citizens and from citizens to institutions. Intransitive power is the community's relationship to itself, its reservoir of shared values. In a democracy it comes from the citizens and is symbolically represented by institutions.

HOST B: And the configuration helps explain crises.

HOST A: Exactly. A crisis happens when the conditions for the configuration stop being met. If institutions fail at steering, if decisions are clearly inadequate or nothing happens at all, you get disillusionment with politics. That can escalate into an integration crisis, when citizens can no longer recognise the shared values in how institutions and their officials behave. Then institutions erode. Göhler mentions the late twentieth century autocracies, but says democracies face this problem constantly. And sometimes institutions adapt through hard reforms instead.

HOST B: The slide "Institutions in context" summed it up as three layers: individual behaviour, institutions, and social order, influencing each other.

HOST A: Now section two: the three "new institutionalisms," from Hall and Taylor in 1996. Göhler mentions them, and the lecture goes deeper.

HOST B: Rational choice institutionalism first.

HOST A: Institutions as rules of the game. The basic idea: people pursue specific goals and use institutions as rules of the game to reach them. Institutions are formal and informal rules that create incentives and stabilise expectations about others' behaviour. The logic of action is calculus, the logic of expected consequences. Institutions arise because they solve problems or benefit everyone involved. In Göhler, this comes from economics: Ronald Coase asked in 1937 why firms exist at all, and the answer was transaction costs. There are also the property rights approach and the principal-agent approach.

HOST B: Sociological institutionalism.

HOST A: Institutions as cultural frameworks. They're like a culture that tells us what's normal, right or even conceivable. Institutions are recurring social practices, conventions, customs, shared ideas and mental models, "the way it's done around here." The logic of action is culture, the logic of appropriateness. So institutions can survive even if they're inefficient. In Göhler, Meyer and Rowan argue that organisations adopt recognised procedures as "facades of rationality" to gain legitimacy. The Carnegie School talks about "uncertainty absorption" and the "garbage can" model of decision-making.

HOST B: Historical institutionalism.

HOST A: Institutions as history that continues to have an impact. The basic idea is path dependence: institutional decisions, once taken, shape the future, often for decades. Institutions are established procedures and routines, formal and informal. The logic is increasing returns on investment, plus critical junctures, which Göhler calls "branching points," moments of crisis when new paths can open. It came out of the "Bringing the State Back In" movement, and it includes veto player theory from George Tsebelis. Göhler's example is German federalism, where the Bundesrat can block government initiatives.

HOST B: And the lecture used one example across all three: the consensus rule in UN climate politics.

HOST A: Rational choice: the veto that consensus gives every state protects its self-interest, even if that's suboptimal for the group. Sociological: consensus is seen as the appropriate "UN way," the legitimate form of multilateralism. Historical: adopting a new voting rule would itself require consensus. That's a lock-in.

HOST B: The appendix compares them further.

HOST A: Concept of institutions: rational choice is often narrower, focused on formal rules, though it can include informal ones. Sociological is broad: symbolic systems, cognitive scripts, moral frameworks. Historical is broad too: formal and informal procedures, routines and conventions. On structure and agency: rational choice uses a calculus approach where actors have fixed preferences and institutions shape incentives and information. Sociological uses a cultural approach where institutions shape what actors want and think is possible. Historical is eclectic, and emphasises power asymmetries, because institutions distribute resources and influence unequally.

HOST B: Strengths and critiques?

HOST A: Rational choice is precise and elegant, but underestimates how culture shapes preferences. Sociological explains why inefficient structures survive, but neglects power and conflict. Historical explains long-term patterns, but is often more descriptive than explanatory, and "path dependence" can stay vague. Göhler also mentions a fourth approach, discursive institutionalism from Vivien Schmidt, which focuses on ideas and discourse.

HOST B: Section three: from Kyoto to Paris. This draws on Allan and colleagues, 2023, "Making the Paris Agreement."

HOST A: Compare the two. The Kyoto Protocol, 1997, had no temperature goal, only the UNFCCC's aim of preventing "dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system." Emission targets applied only to Annex I parties, the industrialised countries: minus five point two percent between 2008 and 2012 compared with 1990, with individual targets per state. Other countries had no quantified targets. It also had market mechanisms like emissions trading, a compliance mechanism, financial support for developing countries, and it needed fifty-five states covering fifty-five percent of emissions to enter into force.

HOST B: And Paris, 2015.

HOST A: A temperature goal of "well below 2 degrees" and "pursuing efforts" to limit warming to 1.5 degrees, plus net zero in Article 4. All parties submit nationally determined contributions, NDCs, every five years, with an obligation to increase ambition. Plus transparency and reporting, a Global Stocktake every five years, market mechanisms under Article 6.4, more emphasis on adaptation and climate finance, and a mechanism for "loss and damage" in Article 8.

HOST B: And Allan and colleagues name five institutional innovations.

HOST A: One, wider participation and more universal expectations. Two, Paris sets goals for the whole global community. Three, parties set their own NDCs, and "self-differentiation" is the rule. Four, Paris is "hybrid": the legal obligations are limited to submitting NDCs and national reports. Five, a "ratchet": ambition has to increase over time.

HOST B: Then the lecture applied Göhler's two functions.

HOST A: On steering, Kyoto was strong, with legally binding, quantified targets. Paris is weak in the traditional sense, with no binding targets, but it has "soft governance" through transparency, monitoring and the Global Stocktake. On integration, it's the reverse. Kyoto was weak: only developed countries had binding targets, and the US, a major emitter, never joined, so its legitimacy declined. Paris is strong: universal participation and self-chosen contributions create ownership and consensus.

HOST B: And the path to Paris, explained with the three perspectives.

HOST A: Stage one, sociological institutionalism. Institutional design is shaped by normative expectations about legitimacy, not just interests. Science-backed temperature targets, two degrees and then 1.5 under pressure from vulnerable states, civil society mobilisation and signals from green businesses defined what counted as an appropriate response. So did norms of international law and human rights, burden-sharing, broad acceptance of market instruments, and state sovereignty, which decides who even gets to negotiate.

HOST B: Stage two, historical institutionalism.

HOST A: Earlier institutions, even failed ones, shape what can be imagined next. The US failure to ratify Kyoto taught everyone that without reliable US participation, any deal is weak. The split of countries into industrialised and developing, the CBDR principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, was a hard legacy from 1992. And even though Copenhagen in 2009, COP 15, failed, it established the "bottom-up" principle that became Paris's foundation.

HOST B: Stage three, rational choice.

HOST A: Cost-benefit calculations by powerful states and their red lines shaped key design choices. The US had the 1997 Byrd-Hagel Resolution as a domestic signal. The EU and vulnerable states wanted an ambitious, legally binding deal. China rejected binding external requirements but accepted self-selected pledges plus climate finance from industrialised countries. And the 2014 to 2015 US-China agreement was strategic coalition-building. The slide also lists the presidency, the High Ambition Coalition and the secretariat as part of the negotiation strategies.

HOST B: And the message?

HOST A: Only a combination of all three perspectives gives a full explanation. The stages roughly, but not neatly, match the three lenses. Some elements can be read in more than one way. But using only one lens wouldn't give you the full picture. Note that the consensus rule is the lecturers' own addition to Allan's model.

HOST B: Key points of the lecture.

HOST A: Institutions matter, including in politics. They structure society by enabling and constraining action, and through symbolic integration. They can be formal or informal. They don't emerge in a vacuum. They're part of institutional configurations. They're relatively stable, but they can change. And they can stabilise or even perpetuate power relations, but they depend on actors' support to survive.

HOST B: And two lessons for climate policy.

HOST A: Lesson one: one way to strengthen climate policy is to lock in climate protection pathways. That's Ulysses tying himself to the mast. Lesson two: another way is to de-institutionalise pollution-heavy pathways.

HOST B: The appendix asks why US climate policy stays relatively weak, with two explanations.

HOST A: Explanation one: national interests, from Sprinz and Vaahtoranta, 1994. Two variables: how vulnerable a country feels to climate change, and how costly it would be to adapt to ambitious goals. High vulnerability and low adaptation costs mean an interest in strict international targets, like Vanuatu. Low perceived vulnerability and high costs mean an interest in weak targets, like the US.

HOST B: Explanation two: domestic institutions.

HOST A: From Finnegan, 2022, and Finnegan and colleagues, 2025. Again two variables. First, insulation from electoral pressure: proportional representation gives more insulation, majoritarian systems give little. Second, scope for compensating the losers of climate policy: high with concerted, corporatist interest representation, low with pluralist interest reconciliation. Proportional representation plus compensation leads to more ambitious targets: Germany, the Nordic states, Austria, Switzerland. Majoritarian plus pluralist leads to weaker targets: the US, but also Canada and Australia, although the UK is a counter-example with fairly high ambition.

HOST B: Quiz. What are Göhler's three conditions for an institution?

HOST A: Rules exist, they're applied, and those affected are aware of them.

HOST B: What are the two functions of political institutions?

HOST A: Steering, or governance, and integration, through symbolic representation.

HOST B: Match the logic to the institutionalism. Logic of appropriateness?

HOST A: Sociological.

HOST B: Logic of expected consequences?

HOST A: Rational choice.

HOST B: Path dependence and critical junctures?

HOST A: Historical.

HOST B: Was Paris stronger or weaker than Kyoto?

HOST A: Trick question. Weaker on traditional steering, because there are no binding targets. Stronger on integration, because of universal participation and ownership.
